\begin{abstract}
Evasion of machine-learning network intrusion detectors is mostly shown in \emph{feature space}, on
flow vectors that may match no traffic an attacker could send. We measure \emph{problem-space}
evasion: every perturbation is sent as real traffic in an emulated network and counted only if a functional
oracle confirms the attack works. In both attack families the attacker
sets the detected signal: timing regularity for command-and-control (C2) beaconing, throughput for data
exfiltration. The detectors are a neural network on two input sets, an
8-qubit variational quantum classifier, Random Forest, XGBoost and a single-feature rule. Of 327 cases
where a detector flagged the unmodified attack, 326 were evaded by working traffic: beacons by a
median $\pm$20--30\% check-in jitter, exfiltration by slowing a 3\,s transfer to about 6\,s against a
60\,s deadline. Over ten training seeds, the quantum classifier had the lowest clean accuracy in 19 of
20 runs and was easier to evade than its matched classical counterpart in 8 of 10 seeds per family.
Adversarial training holds only for the configurations it was shown: payload padding re-breaks every
hardened C2 detector, new transfer volumes or chunking re-break hardened tree ensembles, and tree
ensembles hardened over the whole sampled timing and size range fall to padding just past it. Moving
the threshold stops jittered beacons for the tree ensembles at a 23--24\% benign false-positive rate;
padded beacons or throttled exfiltration need 34--49\%. In a public normal-host capture, 12.3\% of benign flows are as regular as our beacons (41.9\%
in our synthetic mix).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Three recent surveys catalogue adversarial evasion attacks on machine-learning network intrusion
detection systems (NIDS)~\cite{nids_survey2023,ennaji2024,nids_understanding2026}. Most of those attacks
work in \emph{feature space}: the attacker perturbs a flow's feature vector directly, often with
gradient access, and success is measured on the perturbed vector. A perturbed feature vector is not
traffic. It may correspond to no sequence of packets an attacker could send, or to traffic that no
longer performs the attack. Apruzzese et al.~\cite{apruzzese2022} argue for threat models that limit
what the attacker knows and controls, and elShehaby and Matrawy~\cite{elshehaby2023} contend that
evasion against real ML-NIDS remains largely impractical, especially against dynamic ones. This
paper measures whether an attacker can evade a trained detector with traffic it can send, and what
the evasion costs the attack.

The \emph{problem-space} setting of Pierazzi et al.~\cite{pierazzi2020} makes the question precise: the
attacker must produce a real object, and perturbations are limited to transformations that are
realizable and preserve the object's function. For network traffic this is hard to evaluate. The map
from packets to flow features is neither invertible nor differentiable, and a static benchmark dataset
cannot be regenerated with different attacker parameters; public intrusion datasets also carry
documented labelling and representativeness problems~\cite{engelen2021,kenyon2020}. Problem-space evaluation therefore needs a
traffic generator in the loop: each candidate perturbation is sent, captured and re-featurized before
the detector sees it, and its effect on the attack is checked.

We chose two attack families whose detectable signal is set by the attacker. A command-and-control
(C2) beacon checks in with its server at a regular interval, and flow-level beacon detectors key on that
regularity~\cite{yilmaz2026,gardiner2014}. Within the latency an operator tolerates, randomizing the
interval or padding the payload costs the beacon nothing. Data exfiltration, whose attack vectors and
countermeasures Ullah et al.~\cite{ullah2018} review, was picked as the contrast.
Its signal is throughput, and slowing the transfer to evade a rate-based detector costs time, because
the data must still leave before a deadline. Benign networks carry traffic with both shapes: NTP and
health checks are as periodic as a beacon, and backups and downloads move bulk data at high rate. A
detector cannot treat either shape as malicious without flagging that traffic too.

This choice is the test, not a convenience. The regularity our C2 detectors key on is the signal the
beaconing-detection literature treats as a reliable discriminator~\cite{yilmaz2026,gardiner2014}, and
Cobalt Strike exposes check-in jitter as a configuration setting an operator sets before the
beacon ships~\cite{cobaltstrike_beacon}. A detector that rests on a signal the attacker both controls
and is known to vary is the case where realizable evasion should succeed if it can succeed anywhere.
Measuring the cost of that evasion, and whether adversarial training removes it, is what the result
turns on. We do not claim every flow-level detector is as fragile; we show that the ones built on
attacker-set traffic shape are, and quantify by how much.

The second question is about the detector. Abreu et al.~\cite{abreu2024}, Thomos and
Andronikos~\cite{thomos2026} and Gong et al.~\cite{qvcnn2024} apply quantum-classical classifiers to
intrusion detection and report clean accuracy close to classical baselines. West et
al.~\cite{west2023nature,west2023bench} ask whether quantum classifiers resist adversarial perturbation
better than classical ones. Both lines evaluate on static features or with $L_p$-bounded
feature-space perturbations, so neither tests a quantum detector against real attack traffic. We train
a classical multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a variational quantum classifier (VQC) on identical reduced
inputs, and compare both with three detectors common in deployed NIDS: Random Forest, XGBoost, and a
single-feature threshold rule.

All traffic is generated in an emulated Mininet network. A sweep sends real attacks with candidate
parameters, extracts their flow features, and scores them against every detector offline. A functional
oracle admits a perturbation only if the beacon keeps completing check-ins with no gap over 30\,s, or if
the exfiltrated payload reaches the collector within 60\,s. We then harden every detector by
adversarial training, evaluate it on held-out attack instances, and test whether the attacker recovers
by changing a parameter the hardening did not cover.

Every detector was evaded on every attack instance it had flagged, with one exception out of 327. The
median C2 evasion took at most $\pm$30\% check-in jitter, and no evaded beacon needed more than
$\pm$80\%. Exfiltration evasion cut the target send rate by a factor of 2.5 to 3, which added a median of
1.6--2.7\,s to a 3.3\,s transfer against a 60\,s deadline. The tree ensembles were no harder to evade
than the MLP. We retrained every detector with ten model seeds: the VQC was easier to evade than the MLP
trained on its inputs in 8 of 10 seeds on each family, and its clean accuracy was the lowest of the
learned detectors in 19 of the 20 runs. Hardening closed the attacked axis for the trained configuration only. Payload-size padding
re-broke every hardened C2 detector, and hardened tree ensembles that resisted exfiltration throttling
were evaded again once the attacker also changed the transfer volume, chunk timing or chunk size.
Hardening on timing and size together cut timing-jitter evasion to at most 2 of 9 held-out beacons
for the classical detectors and size-jitter evasion to at most 1 of 9 for the tree ensembles. Padding
still evaded the jointly hardened MLPs and VQC in most seeds, and a beacon combining $\pm$30\% jitter
with padding evaded the jointly hardened MLPs and VQC on 6 to 9 of 9 held-out beacons in every seed and
XGBoost on 2 to 7. Training instead on random points across the whole timing and size range stopped
both tree ensembles inside that range, with at most 1 of 9 held-out beacons evading in any seed. Just
outside it, padding past the trained maximum evaded the range-wide XGBoost on 8 or 9 of 9 held-out
beacons in every seed and the Random Forest on 3 to 5.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A problem-space evaluation of flow-level NIDS on two attack families, in which every
        perturbation is sent as real traffic, checked by a functional oracle, and counted as an evasion
        only if the detector caught the unmodified attack.
  \item A comparison of six detectors (an MLP on two input sets, a VQC on matched inputs, Random
        Forest, XGBoost and a single-feature rule) on identical captured flows. To our knowledge this is
        the first problem-space comparison of a quantum and a classical classifier on a security task,
        and we repeat it over ten training seeds.
  \item Evidence from both attack families that adversarial training defends the attack configuration
        it was trained on: changing one further attacker-controlled parameter restores evasion at close
        to the original cost. Hardening on two C2 parameters together leaves combinations of them open
        for the MLPs and the VQC in every seed, and partly open for XGBoost. Hardening on the whole
        range of both parameters closes that range for the tree ensembles, and beacons just past its
        edge evade them again.
  \item A methodological result: evasion queries must reproduce the network conditions of the
        training captures. Exfiltration queries run on an uncontended two-host topology were out of
        distribution, and two detectors called them benign with no perturbation applied.
  \item A released testbed (traffic generators, functional oracles, detectors, sweeps and captured
        data) and a comparison of the synthetic benign mix against a public capture of a real host.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the threat model,
testbed, detectors and evasion procedure. Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the results,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them and their limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This work draws on five lines of research: evaluation practice for machine-learning intrusion
detection, problem-space adversarial machine learning, adversarial evasion of network intrusion
detectors, detection of C2 beaconing, and quantum machine learning, both as an intrusion detector and as
a candidate for adversarial robustness.

\subsection{Evaluation practice for ML-based intrusion detection}
Sommer and Paxson~\cite{sommer2010} argued that intrusion detection differs from other machine-learning
tasks in ways that make lab results transfer poorly, among them the cost of false positives and the
difficulty of obtaining representative traffic. Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022} catalogue ten pitfalls of
machine learning in computer security, including sampling bias, inappropriate performance measures, the
base-rate fallacy and lab-only evaluation. Apruzzese et al.~\cite{apruzzese2023sok} propose assessing
NIDS under the operational factors that decide deployment. Our evaluation is lab-only in Arp et al.'s
sense, since all traffic is emulated. We address the other pitfalls we can: false-positive rates are
reported next to every hardening result and compared at matched values (Section~\ref{sec:op}), the
synthetic benign mix is checked against a real capture (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}), and model results
are repeated over ten training seeds (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}).

\subsection{Problem-space and feature-space adversarial attacks}
Most adversarial-ML results perturb the feature vector directly and measure success in feature space.
Pierazzi et al.~\cite{pierazzi2020} formalized the \emph{problem-space} alternative, in which the
attacker must produce a real object (in their case, an Android app). The perturbation is limited to
available transformations, must preserve the object's semantics, must leave no artifacts a defender
could spot, and must look plausible, because in most domains no inverse map takes a desired feature
vector back to a valid object. Chernikova and Oprea's FENCE~\cite{fence2022} generates evasions for
deep models that respect dependencies between features, with network security among its case studies.
Both works treat realizability and functionality preservation as hard constraints, for malware and for
engineered features. We apply the same constraints to network flow NIDS by putting a traffic generator
in the loop: every candidate perturbation is emitted as packets and admitted only if a functional
oracle confirms the attack still works (Section~\ref{sec:evasion}).

\subsection{Adversarial evasion against NIDS}
Three surveys~\cite{nids_survey2023,ennaji2024,nids_understanding2026} cover adversarial attacks on
ML-based NIDS, and a common criticism in that literature is that reported attacks succeed in feature
space or under threat models no attacker could realize on the wire. Apruzzese et
al.~\cite{apruzzese2022} proposed threat models that limit attacker knowledge and control.
elShehaby and Matrawy~\cite{elshehaby2023} argued that evasion attacks remain impractical against real
ML-NIDS, especially dynamic ones. Catillo et al.~\cite{catillo2024} built explicitly problem-space NIDS
attacks in 2024. Other work restricts perturbations to fields the attacker controls: Li~\cite{li2026}
splits flow features into directly, indirectly and non-controllable groups and perturbs only the
controllable ones, and Jing et al.~\cite{jing2025} evade transformer-based traffic classifiers by
prepending padding bytes that do not disturb transmission. Sheatsley et al.~\cite{sheatsley2022} craft
adversarial examples that obey domain constraints learned from network data. Two lines of work change
traffic rather than feature vectors: Rigaki and Garcia~\cite{rigaki2018} used a generative adversarial
network to adapt malware communication so that it avoided detection, and Han et al.~\cite{han2021}
studied gray- and black-box traffic-space attacks that mutate original traffic against ML-based NIDS.
On the defense side, Apruzzese et al.~\cite{apruzzese2020rf} proposed a method for hardening
random-forest detectors against adversarial perturbations.

We agree with the realism critique and test it on one attack class where it should fail. In C2
beaconing, the detectable signal (timing and size regularity) is a set of parameters the attacker
already controls at no cost to the beacon. There, evasion succeeded against all but one of 160
detected (detector, instance) pairs, with the functional oracle confirming that every evading beacon
kept checking in. Li's controllable features appear in our setup as the timing-jitter and size-jitter
parameters of the beacon generator. The difference is that our generator must emit valid traffic, so
a perturbation that cannot be sent is never scored. This also lets us measure three quantities a
feature-space attack cannot: the minimal realizable evading perturbation, the number of black-box
queries to evasion, and whether the perturbed attack still functions. Where the traffic-space attacks
above search for an evading sample, we sweep the attacker's parameters on a fixed grid, so the cost of
evasion is measured per detector on identical traffic.

\subsection{Detecting C2 beaconing}
A beacon checks in at a near-constant interval, so flow-level beacon detectors look for timing
regularity. Yilmaz et al.~\cite{yilmaz2026} use a low coefficient of variation (CV) of inter-arrival
times, combined with anomaly detectors, and Gardiner et al.~\cite{gardiner2014} survey C2 techniques and
defences as of 2014. Our \texttt{cv\_iat} feature is this CV (Section~\ref{sec:features}), and it is the
signal we attack. Because the check-in interval is set by the attacker and a beacon tolerates several
seconds of slack, raising jitter costs the beacon nothing. Benign NTP, health checks and keepalives are
as regular as a naive beacon, which limits how far a detector can tighten its threshold before it
flags them.

Earlier C2 detectors look at many connections over long periods rather than one flow. BotFinder
\cite{tegeler2012} detects bots from statistics of their network traffic without inspecting payloads,
Disclosure~\cite{bilge2012} identifies C2 servers from large-scale NetFlow records, and
BAYWATCH~\cite{hu2016baywatch} detects beaconing hosts in enterprise-scale networks. These systems
aggregate behaviour over horizons our 60\,s window does not cover (Section~\ref{sec:window}); we did not
evaluate them.

\subsection{Quantum machine learning for intrusion detection}
Variational quantum classifiers, quantum SVMs and quantum CNNs have been applied to intrusion
detection, with reported accuracy close to or above classical
baselines~\cite{abreu2024,thomos2026,qvcnn2024}; Sai et al.~\cite{qml_sec_taxonomy2025} give a taxonomy
of quantum machine learning for security. These studies report clean accuracy on static feature
datasets and do not test evasion. Our VQC uses the circuit-centric classifier design of Schuld et
al.~\cite{schuld2020}. Variational circuits are known to be hard to train~\cite{cerezo2021}, and a single
training run can misstate a model family, which is one reason we repeat every model over ten seeds. We train a VQC and a classical MLP on matched inputs
(Section~\ref{sec:detectors}) and compare them on the same captured attack traffic.

\subsection{Adversarial robustness of quantum classifiers}
Outside intrusion detection, a separate literature asks whether quantum classifiers resist
adversarial examples better than classical ones. Lu et al.~\cite{lu2020} and Liao et
al.~\cite{liao2021} showed that VQCs are vulnerable to adversarial examples, and Liu and
Wittek~\cite{liu2020} showed that the perturbation needed to misclassify a quantum classifier shrinks as
the dimension of its Hilbert space grows. West et
al.~\cite{west2023nature} reported that quantum models can learn features that classical adversarial
attacks do not disturb, and later benchmarked adversarially robust quantum models at
scale~\cite{west2023bench}; Kustiawan and Ghauth~\cite{qml_robust_review2026} review the area. All of
these measure robustness to $L_p$-bounded perturbations of the encoded input, which need not correspond
to any input an attacker could produce.

To our knowledge, ours is the first comparison of a quantum and a classical classifier on a security
task in which robustness is measured against realized, functionality-preserving attack traffic. Over ten
training seeds, the VQC needed less perturbation than the matched MLP on 157 of 249 paired C2 instances
and more on 49, and on exfiltration less on 119 of 257 and more on 48
(Section~\ref{sec:seeds}).

\subsection{Adversarial training and its limits}
Adversarial training~\cite{madry2018} is the standard defense and has been studied for
NIDS~\cite{apruzzese2020rf,ennaji2024,nids_advtrain2026}. Tram\`{e}r and Boneh~\cite{tramer2019} showed
that robustness gained against one perturbation type does not carry over to others and that robustness
to several types trades off, and Maini et al.~\cite{maini2020} train against the union of several
perturbation models. Those results concern $L_p$-bounded and spatial perturbations of images. We
observe the same pattern in the problem space, where each perturbation type is a parameter of the
attack traffic. Hardening against timing jitter cut
held-out jitter evasion to 0 of 9 for four of the five learned C2 detectors, and payload-size padding
then re-broke every one of them on every instance it detected (Section~\ref{sec:defense}). On
exfiltration, hardened tree ensembles that stopped throttled transfers were evaded again once the
attacker changed the chunk size (Section~\ref{sec:exfil-results}). Training on both C2 axes at once
held against the combined attack only for the Random Forest (Section~\ref{sec:joint}). Training on
random points across the whole range of both axes held the tree ensembles inside that range, and
payload padding past it evaded them again (Section~\ref{sec:box}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the C2 study in full (Sections~\ref{sec:threat}--\ref{sec:defense-method}) and
then the changes made for exfiltration (Section~\ref{sec:exfil-method}). Code and captured data are
listed under Data availability.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}
The attacker has compromised an internal host and runs a command-and-control (C2) beacon on it: the
host periodically checks in with an attacker-controlled server to receive commands. A NIDS observes
traffic at the flow level and classifies each flow as benign or C2. The attacker wants to evade
detection while keeping the control channel working. The attacker:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{can} manipulate the realizable parameters of their own traffic: the check-in interval,
        the timing jitter (randomness of the interval), the payload size, and the size jitter. These
        are degrees of freedom a real C2 implementation controls and that do not, within bounds,
        impair the beacon;
  \item \textbf{cannot} alter other hosts' traffic, the detector's parameters, or the
        feature-extraction pipeline, and cannot submit feature vectors directly; every change must be
        sent as traffic on the wire;
  \item \textbf{can} query the detector in a black-box manner (observe benign/malicious verdicts),
        modelling an attacker who tests candidate configurations against a deployed or surrogate
        detector.
\end{itemize}
This is a \emph{problem-space} threat model~\cite{pierazzi2020}: perturbations are limited to traffic the
attacker can send without breaking the attack. A perturbation is \emph{admissible} only if the
resulting beacon remains functional (Section~\ref{sec:evasion}).

\subsection{Testbed and traffic generation}
\label{sec:testbed}
A static benchmark dataset cannot be regenerated with new attacker parameters, so we emulate a network
in Mininet~\cite{mininet2010}. Hosts attach to a single Open vSwitch switch via \texttt{TCLink} links
capped at 10\,Mbit/s, which bounds the volume each 60\,s capture records. On every host we disable NIC offloads (\texttt{gso}, \texttt{tso},
\texttt{gro}, \texttt{lro}) so the capture records real MTU-sized packets rather than offload
super-segments, and we pre-populate ARP tables (\texttt{staticArp}) so address resolution does not
appear as stray flows. Traffic is captured with \texttt{tcpdump} inside the relevant host's network
namespace at a 128-byte snap length (headers only); per-packet size features use the recorded wire
length rather than the truncated capture length.

Each scenario runs for 60\,s and is repeated with per-host, per-run randomized parameters to produce a
dataset. Host h1 is the victim and collector and runs the services; each remaining host generates
traffic in one of the roles below.

\paragraph{C2 beacons (malicious).} A beacon sends a single UDP datagram check-in to a C2 endpoint
every \texttt{interval} seconds ($\pm$\,\texttt{jitter}), with payload size \texttt{payload\_size}
($\pm$\,\texttt{size\_jitter}), and reads back a reply. Because each check-in is one datagram, as in
DNS- or heartbeat-style C2, a flow's packet inter-arrival times are its check-in intervals, and TCP
handshakes and retransmissions do not blur them. A naive beacon is rigid: low \texttt{jitter} and low
\texttt{size\_jitter}.

\paragraph{Benign traffic.} Real networks carry benign traffic as periodic as a beacon: NTP, health
checks, keepalives and telemetry. We generate five benign roles: (a) \emph{rigid} periodic traffic in
the same timing and size band as the beacons, standing in for NTP and strict health checks; (b)
moderately regular keepalives; (c) loose, irregular polling; (d) bursty short TCP application sessions
across several ports, some of which fail; and (e) bulk transfer. Role~(a) is a confuser. Its jitter
bands match the beacon's (Table~\ref{tab:scenario}), so no detector can separate it from a naive beacon,
and clean accuracy stays below 100\% (Section~\ref{sec:clean}). Beacons and roles (a)--(c) come from
one generator that differs only in its jitter and size settings, and each host's role sets the label of
its flows.

\paragraph{Realism check.} To test whether real benign traffic is as regular as our confuser, we ran a
public capture of a normal host~\cite{ctunormal29} through the same pipeline: the host's outbound packets
over two hours, cut into 60\,s windows to match the scenario window (7{,}456 flows with at least five
packets). Of these real flows, 12.3\% are as regular as our C2 beacons ($\texttt{cv\_iat} \le 0.111$, the
95th percentile of the C2 class; Fig.~\ref{fig:realism}). Our synthetic benign mix puts 41.9\% of flows
there, $3.4\times$ as many, so the clean false-positive rates we report are higher than a detector would
see on this host's traffic. Two qualifications apply. The reference host only browses the web, with no
NTP, health checks or monitoring agents, and an enterprise segment with those services would have more
regular benign flows. Part of the real low-\texttt{cv\_iat} mass is also evenly paced bulk transfer
rather than periodic check-ins, which a timing-regularity feature cannot tell apart from a beacon
either. The evasion results do not depend on this density, because evasion moves the beacon into the
benign region whatever its population.

\subsection{Flow features and labeling}
\label{sec:features}
Packets are aggregated into flows keyed by the tuple (protocol, source MAC, destination) rather than
the 5-tuple. With the 5-tuple, an attacker who varies source ports or spoofs addresses splits one
activity into many single-packet flows. The generators never spoof the sender's hardware address, so
keying on it collapses each host's activity into one flow with usable aggregate statistics and turns
port diversity into a within-flow feature. Each flow is labeled by the role of its source MAC; the
collector host's own traffic is excluded.

Each flow is summarized by a vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$ of the features in
Table~\ref{tab:features}; \texttt{iat} denotes inter-arrival time. Writing $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_m$ for a
flow's inter-arrival times with mean $\mu_{\mathrm{IAT}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{IAT}}$,
the timing-regularity feature is their coefficient of variation,
\begin{equation}
\texttt{cv\_iat} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IAT}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{IAT}}}.
\label{eq:cviat}
\end{equation}
A rigid beacon has $\texttt{cv\_iat}\approx 0$ at any interval, while jittered benign traffic has
$\texttt{cv\_iat}=\mathcal{O}(1)$. Unlike \texttt{std\_iat}, the ratio does not grow with the interval,
so a slow rigid beacon and a fast rigid beacon get the same value.

\subsection{Detectors and the matched-input protocol}
\label{sec:detectors}
The MLP and the VQC are trained on the same binary task (benign vs.\ C2) and the same inputs. Three
reference detectors, described at the end of this subsection, stand in for what a defender would run.

\paragraph{Classical MLP.} A multilayer perceptron implemented from scratch in NumPy (no ML
frameworks): two hidden layers of 32 and 16 units with ReLU activations and a 2-way softmax output,
trained by mini-batch gradient descent with Adam (learning rate 0.01, batch size 64, up to 150 epochs
with early stopping, patience 20) under class-balanced loss weighting. The softmax + cross-entropy
gradient was verified against finite differences.

\paragraph{Quantum-classical VQC.} A variational quantum classifier implemented in
PennyLane~\cite{pennylane2018}: an \texttt{AngleEmbedding} of the (PCA-reduced) features onto 8 qubits,
followed by 4 \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers}, PennyLane's implementation of the circuit-centric
classifier layers of Schuld et al.~\cite{schuld2020}, reading out one Pauli-$Z$ expectation per class from
the first two wires, passed through a softmax and trained with cross-entropy (learning rate 0.05, batch
size 16, up to 30 epochs, patience 8). Training uses exact gradients via the \texttt{default.qubit}
state-vector simulator.
Concretely, the PCA features $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}$ are angle-encoded as single-qubit $R_Y$
rotations $U(\mathbf{z})$, a trainable \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers} block $V(\boldsymbol{\Theta})$
is applied, and one Pauli-$Z$ expectation is read from each of the first two wires,
\begin{equation}
f_i(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\Theta}) \;=\;
\langle 0|\,U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{z})\,V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\,Z_i\,V(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\,U(\mathbf{z})\,|0\rangle,
\qquad i\in\{0,1\},
\label{eq:vqc}
\end{equation}
yielding class probabilities $P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})=\mathrm{softmax}\big(2\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\Theta})\big)$.
The circuit has $4\times 8\times 3 = 96$ trainable parameters.

\paragraph{Matched inputs.} So that a difference between the MLP and the VQC comes from the model and
not from the information it receives, both are trained on the same inputs. Raw features are
standardized with training-split statistics and projected onto their first eight principal
components, one per qubit,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} \;=\; \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{PCA}}^{\top}\big((\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})/\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{8},
\label{eq:pca}
\end{equation}
where the division is per feature. The eight components retain 99.6\% of the variance. For the VQC each
component is then mapped affinely into the $R_Y$ angle range that \texttt{AngleEmbedding} requires; a
monotone per-feature map adds no information. We also train the MLP on all 17 features, which we call
the native MLP, as the version a defender would deploy. Every preprocessing transform is fit on the
training split only. The dataset comprises 1{,}260 flows (900 benign, 360 C2) from 180 scenario runs,
split 70/15/15 into train/validation/test and stratified by class.

A detector outputs a posterior $P(\text{benign}\mid\cdot)$
and flags a flow as malicious when
\begin{equation}
P(\text{benign}\mid\cdot) < \tau, \qquad \tau = 0.5 .
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:detectors} summarizes the two detectors and Table~\ref{tab:dataset} the dataset split.

\paragraph{Reference detectors.} We also train three reference detectors on the same split and all 17
features: a Random Forest (300 trees, class-balanced), gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, depth 4,
early-stopped on the validation split), and a single-feature threshold rule. The rule's feature is
\texttt{cv\_iat} for C2 and \texttt{bytes\_per\_second} for exfiltration, and its cut is the one that
maximizes balanced accuracy on the training split.
These three use library implementations (scikit-learn, XGBoost) and are not part of the matched-input
comparison. All six detectors are scored with the same decision rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:decision}).

\subsection{Problem-space evasion}
\label{sec:evasion}
The evasion is a model-in-the-loop search for the smallest realizable, functionality-preserving
perturbation that flips the detector's verdict to benign.

\paragraph{Query primitive.} A single query takes a parameter vector
\[
\theta = (\texttt{interval},\ \texttt{jitter},\ \texttt{payload\_size},\ \texttt{size\_jitter}),
\]
runs a beacon with those parameters in a 60\,s Mininet capture (the training window length), extracts
the beacon's flow, and returns its 17-feature vector. Each query runs inside the full training scenario
of Table~\ref{tab:scenario}: the controlled beacon replaces one of the two scenario beacons on h2 and
checks in with the scenario's echo server, while the other beacon and every benign host run as
background. Background parameters are drawn once per instance seed, so every grid point of an instance
sees the same background, and every host except the controlled beacon is excluded from scoring by
source MAC. The query beacon therefore shares the capture link with the same traffic as a training
beacon. An earlier C2 sweep on a two-host topology without background traffic gave the same median
evading jitter for both MLPs and the VQC, and a median 0.025 higher for the Random Forest; for
exfiltration the topology changed the result (Section~\ref{sec:exfil-method}).

\paragraph{Functional oracle.} During each query the beacon logs the timestamp of every check-in whose
reply came back. The beacon is \emph{functional} if it completed at least two such check-ins and no
silent period, counting the stretches before the first and after the last check-in, exceeded a
tolerable control latency $T_{\max}=30$\,s (\texttt{MAX\_INTERVAL}). Writing $t_1<\dots<t_C$ for the
timestamps of the successful check-ins and $t_0$, $t_{C+1}$ for the start and end of the capture window,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Func}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,C \ge 2 \;\wedge\; \max_{0\le k\le C}(t_{k+1}-t_k) \le T_{\max}\,\right].
\label{eq:oracle}
\end{equation}
Including the window edges catches a beacon that falls silent partway through the capture. A
perturbation that silences the beacon or stretches a gap past 30\,s is inadmissible.

\paragraph{Line search.} For a given detector and axis, we fix the other parameters and sweep the axis
over an ascending grid. Let $\Phi(\theta)$ denote running a beacon with parameters $\theta$ as real
traffic and extracting its 17-feature vector, and let $\theta\oplus\delta\mathbf{e}_a$ increment axis
$a$ by $\delta$. For each beacon instance the minimal evading perturbation is
\begin{equation}
\delta^{\star} \;=\; \min\big\{\,\delta\ge 0 \;:\;
P(\text{benign}\mid\Phi(\theta\oplus\delta\mathbf{e}_a)) \ge \tau \;\wedge\; \mathrm{Func}(\theta\oplus\delta\mathbf{e}_a)=1\,\big\},
\label{eq:evasion}
\end{equation}
the smallest functionality-preserving move along $a$ that flips the verdict (Eq.~\ref{eq:decision}). We
report the evasion rate, the distribution of $\delta^{\star}$, and the number of grid points queried
before evasion.

A \emph{beacon instance} is a (configuration, seed) pair. The configuration fixes the check-in
interval, with a 64\,B payload and zero size jitter, and the seed is an independent RNG draw within it.
We sweep three intervals ($2$, $4$, $8$\,s) with ten seeds each, $n=30$ instances in all.

\paragraph{Detected-only scoring.} An instance counts toward a detector's evasion rate only if that
detector flagged the unmodified attack (the grid floor, e.g.\ jitter $0$). A detector that calls the
naive attack benign has failed at detection, not been evaded, so we report its naive detection rate
next to every evasion rate. The detailed results use the default $\tau=0.5$;
Section~\ref{sec:op} moves $\tau$ to compare detectors at matched false-positive rates. Along the
jitter axis we report the minimal evading perturbation as a bracket $(j_{-},\,j_{+}]$: the largest grid
value that did not evade and the smallest that did. We search the timing-jitter axis first
(Section~\ref{sec:jitter}) and the payload-size-jitter axis against hardened detectors
(Section~\ref{sec:defense}). Neither axis prevents a check-in.

\subsection{Paired transferability}
\label{sec:paired-method}
To compare detectors on identical traffic and to measure cross-model transfer, we separate capture
from scoring. A \emph{capture-once sweep} runs the full jitter grid for every beacon instance one time
and saves each flow's features and functional verdict, with no detector in the loop. Every evasion
number we report comes from scoring these saved flows offline against each detector: (a) each
detector's evasion rate and minimal evading jitter (Section~\ref{sec:jitter}), (b) a paired comparison
of minimal evading jitter on the same flows, and (c) transfer, meaning whether a beacon tuned to just
evade one detector also evades another. The same flows are reused for the adversarially trained
models, so capture noise does not differ between any two detectors being compared.

\subsection{Training-seed variation}
\label{sec:seeds-method}
A single training run cannot separate a property of a model family from the luck of one initialisation.
We therefore retrain every learned detector with model seeds 0 to 9, keeping the data split fixed at the
paper's split. The seed sets the MLP and VQC initial weights and batch order and the Random Forest and
XGBoost random state; the single-feature rule has no random component. Each seed covers the clean and
single-axis hardened detectors of both families and the jointly and range-wide hardened C2 detectors,
and every seed is scored on the
same saved sweeps with the same conventions. Seed 0 reproduces the detailed tables, which report it.

\subsection{Adversarial-training defense and axis escalation}
\label{sec:defense-method}
We fold evaded beacons from the sweep into the training set, labeled C2, and retrain each detector. To
avoid leakage we split by instance: a stratified two-thirds goes into training and the rest is held out
(21 training and 9 held-out instances, three per interval). We report each hardened detector's clean
accuracy and benign false-positive rate, because teaching it that jittered beacons are malicious can
make it flag the rigid benign host. Evaluation then has two steps. First, we re-score the held-out
jitter evasions to see whether the original attack still succeeds. Second, because the hardening data
varies only timing, we run an \emph{axis escalation}: with timing jitter held at a value that evaded
the unhardened detector, we run the line search on payload-size jitter, which the hardening data never
varied. This tests whether hardening on one axis closed the vulnerability or moved it to another axis
that is just as cheap for the attacker.

A second defense round, \emph{joint hardening}, folds in the functional beacons from both sweeps for
the same 21 training instances: the timing-jitter sweep and a payload-size-jitter sweep run at timing
jitter $0.5$. Every detector is retrained from the clean training split plus these beacons, labeled C2.
We score the jointly hardened detectors on the 9 held-out instances along three lines: timing jitter
alone, payload-size jitter at timing jitter $0.5$, and payload-size jitter at timing jitter $0.3$. The
third line passes through combinations that neither round trained on, since the size sweep in the
training data was run only at jitter $0.5$.

A third round, \emph{range-wide hardening}, replaces the size line with points spread over the whole
region the two sweeps span. For each of the 30 instances we capture 8 beacons at (timing jitter, size
jitter) points drawn by Latin hypercube sampling over $[0, 0.8] \times [0, 0.6]$: each axis is cut into 8
equal intervals and each interval receives one point. The 21 training instances contribute 168 such
beacons, the number the size line contributed to joint hardening, and every detector is retrained on
the clean split, the timing-jitter beacons and these 168. The 72 points of the 9 held-out instances form
its test set. These points come from the region the round trained on, which favours it over the
single-axis and joint rounds, so two further held-out sweeps of 8 Latin-hypercube points per instance
sample outside that region: timing jitter in $[0.8, 2.0]$ with size jitter in $[0, 1.0]$, and size
jitter in $[0.6, 1.0]$ with timing jitter in $[0, 0.8]$. Above timing jitter 1 some sleeps reach zero
and check-ins go out back to back; size jitter 1.0 spreads the 64-byte payload over 1 to 128 bytes. The
oracle judged every capture, and all 384 beacons in these three sweeps were functional. For these
random-point sweeps we report, over the instances a detector flags unmodified, how many held-out
instances have at least one evading point. Points of one instance share its seed, so the instance is
the unit of count; the number of evading points shows how much of a region is open.

\subsection{Second attack family: data exfiltration}
\label{sec:exfil-method}
Exfiltration differs from C2 in two ways. Its detectable signal is throughput rather than timing
regularity, and its evasion, throttling, costs the attacker time against a delivery deadline. The
testbed, flow features, detectors and matched-input protocol
(Sections~\ref{sec:testbed}--\ref{sec:detectors}) are reused unchanged; the attack, its benign
confuser and the evasion axis are new.

\paragraph{Threat model.} The attacker has staged a fixed body of data on a compromised internal host
and must move it to an external collector; the NIDS classifies each flow as benign or exfiltration.
The attacker \textbf{can} set the transfer rate, the chunk size and the inter-chunk timing, but
\textbf{cannot} reduce the volume that must leave, alter other hosts' traffic, or touch the detector. A
perturbation is admissible only if the full payload arrives within a deadline (the functional oracle
below).

\paragraph{Traffic and benign confuser.} One generator produces exfiltration as a single TCP transfer
of a fixed \texttt{volume} to a collector, paced to a target rate by inter-chunk delays. A \emph{naive}
exfiltration sends the payload as fast as it can, producing a short, high-throughput flow. The main
confuser is high-rate benign bulk transfer (backups, large downloads, database replication), drawn from
the same volume and rate bands as naive exfiltration, so a flow-level detector cannot separate the two
without flagging benign bulk. The benign mix also includes low-rate trickle and moderate transfers and
the background roles of Section~\ref{sec:testbed}. As in C2, the only systematic difference between the
attack and its nearest benign class is the quantity the evasion changes, here throughput. The
exfiltration dataset comprises 1{,}440 flows (1{,}080 benign, 360 exfil) from 180 scenario runs, split
70/15/15 and stratified.

\paragraph{Query topology.} An exfiltration flow's features depend on link contention, because in the
training scenario the exfiltration shares the capture host's 10\,Mbit/s link with benign bulk
transfers; unthrottled exfiltrations in our evasion sweep moved a median of 321\,KB/s against a nominal
600\,KB/s. As for C2, exfiltration queries run inside the full training scenario, with background
parameters drawn once per instance seed, and the controlled exfiltration sends to its own collector
port so the delivery check counts only its bytes. Running these queries on a two-host topology instead
produced out-of-distribution flows (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); we report only full-topology results.
We sweep three volumes ($0.8$, $1.15$, $1.5$\,MB) with ten seeds each ($n=30$) at a naive rate of
$600$\,KB/s.

\paragraph{Evasion axis and functional oracle.} The evasion axis is the throttle $c\ge 0$: the
attacker lowers the transfer rate to move \texttt{bytes\_per\_second} out of the high-rate band. The
effective rate is
\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{eff}} \;=\; \frac{r_{0}}{1+c},
\label{eq:throttle}
\end{equation}
so $c=0$ is the naive transfer and $c=1$ halves the rate. The line search of Section~\ref{sec:evasion}
sweeps $c$ upward for the minimal evading value, as it does for jitter. The oracle is where the two
families differ. Raising jitter costs a beacon nothing, but throttling stretches the transfer, and the
payload must still arrive in time. Writing $b(t)$ for the cumulative bytes delivered by time $t$, with
volume $V$, deadline $D$, and transfer start $t_0$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Func}_{\mathrm{exfil}} \;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\exists\, t \le t_0 + D :\; b(t) \ge V\,\right],
\label{eq:exfil-oracle}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the full payload must arrive within the deadline ($D=60$\,s), with delivery confirmed at the
collector. A transfer throttled far enough cannot finish in time, so here the functional constraint can
defeat the evasion: a 1.5\,MB payload misses the deadline below about 25\,KB/s.

\paragraph{Defense for exfiltration.} Adversarial training follows Section~\ref{sec:defense-method}:
functional throttled-exfiltration flows from 21 instances are folded into training as exfil, and the 9
held-out instances are re-scored. To test what a hardened detector has learned, we also run escalation
sweeps on volumes unseen in hardening, changing one further transfer parameter at a time (chunk
jitter, chunk size).

\subsection{Clean detection performance}

\subsection{Problem-space evasion via timing jitter}
\label{sec:jitter}

minimal jitter at which the beacon is classified benign while remaining functional, over the 30 beacon
instances (three check-in intervals $\times$ ten seeds). Every captured configuration passed the
functional oracle ($\geq 2$ successful check-ins, no gap $> 30$\,s).

Each detector was evaded on every beacon it detected, except for one VQC instance
(Table~\ref{tab:jitter}). The MLPs and the VQC were evaded at a median jitter in $(0.25, 0.3]$, which
randomizes each check-in interval by at most $\pm30\%$. The Random Forest, XGBoost and the rule were

median hides a tail: 7 of 30 beacons against the native MLP, 9 of 30 against the matched MLP and 8 of 19

Forest miss all ten rigid beacons at the $2$\,s interval, which sits at the lower edge of the training
distribution (the 1st percentile of C2 inter-arrival times is 2.1\,s), and detect every beacon at $4$
and $8$\,s. In feature space, raising jitter moves the beacon out of its low-\texttt{cv\_iat} corner
into the region populated by benign flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:decisionspace}). Cobalt Strike exposes
check-in jitter as a configuration setting~\cite{cobaltstrike_beacon}, and every jittered beacon in the
sweep kept reaching its C2 server.

\subsection{Classical vs.\ quantum: paired comparison and transferability}
\label{sec:paired}
The matched MLP and the VQC are trained on identical inputs (Section~\ref{sec:detectors}) and scored on
the same captured flows (Section~\ref{sec:paired-method}), so a difference in evasion threshold between
them reflects the model.

\paragraph{Paired minimal evading jitter.} On the 19 instances that both the matched MLP and the VQC
detected and were evaded on, the VQC required more jitter in 9, the MLP in 8, and they tied at grid
resolution in 2. Seed 0 is the closest of the ten training seeds: in 8 of the 10 the VQC was evaded at
the lower jitter on most paired instances (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}).

also evade the VQC on instances the VQC detected; of those crafted against the VQC, 15 of 19 also evade

threshold can sit one grid step short of the other's, and these two rates vary widely across training
seeds (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}). Transfer to the deployed-style detectors is stable: evasions tuned
against the native MLP also evade the Random Forest on 20 of 20, XGBoost on 30 of 30 and the rule on
30 of 30 instances they detected. An attacker therefore needs no query access to the deployed detector:
tuning against a surrogate MLP suffices for these three.

\subsection{Joint timing and size hardening}
\label{sec:joint}
The escalation above leaves open whether a detector trained on both axes at once would hold. We
retrained every detector on the clean split plus the functional beacons from both the timing-jitter
and the size-jitter sweeps of the 21 training instances (Section~\ref{sec:defense-method}) and scored
the 9 held-out instances (Table~\ref{tab:joint}). Size jitter is swept up to $0.6$, which spreads the
64\,B payload over 26 to 102\,B; training beacons carry 32 to 128\,B. Timing jitter is swept up to $0.8$. A detector that resists to the top of a grid is resistant only up to
that value.

In the seed-0 models (Table~\ref{tab:joint}), joint hardening closes timing jitter: at most 1 of 9
held-out beacons evades any learned detector, and the one that evades the MLPs needs jitter $0.8$. The
tree ensembles also stop the size sweep at jitter $0.5$ (Random Forest 0 of 9, XGBoost 1 of 9), with the
same counts in all ten training seeds. The MLPs and the VQC do not. Padding still evades them on 7 of 9
beacons, at a median size jitter of $0.4$--$0.6$, against $0.1$--$0.3$ for their single-axis versions
(Table~\ref{tab:sizeaxis}). This line varies most across seeds: the jointly hardened native MLP is
evaded on 0 to 8 of its detected beacons depending on the seed, the matched MLP on 1 to 9, the VQC on
5 to 8.

The third column is the test of whether joint hardening learned the attack or the two lines it was
trained on. With timing jitter held at $0.3$, between the trained points, and size jitter swept
upward, every single-axis hardened learned detector is evaded on 9 of 9 beacons at a median size jitter
of $0.05$--$0.3$. Joint hardening raises that cost to $0.15$--$0.6$ but stops the evasion only for the
Random Forest, which is evaded on 2 of 9 in every seed. The jointly hardened MLPs and VQC are evaded on
6 to 9 of 9 in every seed. XGBoost is evaded on 7 of 9 at seed 0 but on 2 to 7 across seeds (median 4),
so for XGBoost joint hardening partly holds.

Relative to single-axis hardening, joint hardening lowers the learned detectors' accuracy by 0 to 2.1
points at seed 0. Its effect on the benign FPR is clearer across seeds than at seed 0, where it rose for
three detectors and fell for two. Over ten seeds it rose in 9 seeds for the native MLP (median
+2.2 points), 9 for the Random Forest (+1.5), 8 for the VQC (+1.1) and 6 for the matched MLP (+0.7), and
fell in 6 seeds for XGBoost ($-1.1$). The \texttt{cv\_iat} rule moves its cut far enough to flag jitter-$0.5$ beacons, which costs it a
benign FPR of 0.674, and it is still evaded by every held-out beacon at jitter $0.8$.

\subsection{Range-wide hardening}
\label{sec:box}
Joint hardening trained on two lines through the timing--size plane, and the held-out random points
show where it left gaps (Table~\ref{tab:box}). Inside the swept region the jointly hardened MLPs let
through 32 and 36 of 72 points, on every held-out instance in every seed. The gap sits in a corner
neither training line reaches: of the 19 points with timing jitter below $0.4$ and size jitter of $0.3$
or more, 17 evade each MLP. The jointly hardened tree ensembles let through 2 (Random Forest) and 5
(XGBoost) of the 72.

Range-wide hardening adds the same number of beacons as joint hardening, spread over the region instead
of along the size line, and it stops the tree ensembles inside the region. At seed 0 XGBoost lets
through 0 of 72 points and the Random Forest 1, and in every seed at most 1 of 9 held-out instances
evades either. It also closes the combined line of Table~\ref{tab:joint} for both, at 1 of 9 (median
over seeds 0.11 for both, against 0.44 and 0.22 after joint hardening). The cost in false positives is
small: the median benign FPR over seeds is 0.163 for XGBoost and 0.178 for the Random Forest, against
0.156 and 0.170 after joint hardening. The other learned detectors do not close. The matched MLP and the
VQC are evaded on 6 to 9 of 9 held-out instances in every seed, and the native MLP on none in four
seeds, one in one, and 6 to 8 in the other five.

The test above favours this round, because its test points come from the region it trained on. The
outside-range sweeps remove that advantage (Fig.~\ref{fig:range}). Past the size edge, with timing jitter
still inside the trained range, 43 of 72 points evade the range-wide XGBoost at seed 0, on 9 of 9
held-out instances, and 8 or 9 of 9 in every seed. Past the timing edge alone it holds better, at 3 of
44 points and one instance, but 20 of the 28 points past both edges evade it. The Random Forest degrades
less: 17 of 72 points past the size edge (4 instances; 3 to 5 across seeds), 5 of 44 past the timing edge
alone, and 22 of 28 past both. Joint hardening does no better outside the region, with 49 of 72 points
past the size edge evading XGBoost and 16 evading the Random Forest. Range-wide training moved the tree
ensembles' boundary to the edge of the region they were trained on, and the attacker only has to step
past that edge: on payload size for XGBoost, on both parameters for the Random Forest. All 144 beacons
outside the region were functional.

\subsection{Matched false-positive rates}
\label{sec:op}
Every result above uses $\tau=0.5$, at which the learned detectors flag 16--19\% of benign C2 test flows
and 14--19\% of benign exfiltration test flows. A defender can flag more. For each detector we set
$\tau$ on the validation split so that a target fraction of benign validation flows is flagged, sweep the
target from 0 to 60\%, and rescore the saved sweeps. An attack instance gets through if any functional
flow the attacker can send scores $P(\text{benign})\ge\tau$; an instance the detector misses unmodified
gets through at no cost. Fig.~\ref{fig:op} plots the share of the 30 instances that get through against
the benign FPR reached on the test split.

On C2 the threshold separates the tree ensembles from the other detectors. At a benign FPR of 23--24\%,
about 5 points above the default, no beacon gets past the Random Forest or XGBoost by timing jitter
alone, up to the grid top of $\pm$80\%. Jitter leaves the payload size fixed, and padding it, the
second free parameter, restores evasion: at a benign FPR of 25\% or below, beacons that may also pad
their payload get past the Random Forest in 26 of 30 instances and past XGBoost in 29. The Random Forest
stops padded beacons only at a 34\% FPR, and XGBoost still lets 14 of 30 through at 40\%. At 40\% FPR the
MLPs let 11 to 17 of 30 beacons through, at jitter up to $0.8$, and the VQC 28 to 29. The \texttt{cv\_iat} rule
lets every beacon through at every threshold up to 60\% FPR, because jittered beacons and loose benign
polling have the same \texttt{cv\_iat}.

On exfiltration, the MLPs, XGBoost and the throughput rule let at most 3 of 30 throttled transfers
through only from a benign FPR of 44--49\%. The VQC lets every transfer through at every threshold tested.
The Random Forest's scores are coarse enough that it cannot be set above a 25\% FPR, where 21 of 30
transfers get through.

\subsection{Variation across training seeds}
\label{sec:seeds}
Table~\ref{tab:seeds} repeats the main measurements over model seeds 0 to 9
(Section~\ref{sec:seeds-method}). The evasion results do not depend on the seed. In every seed, the
MLPs, Random Forest and XGBoost were evaded on every C2 beacon they detected, apart from one beacon
against the matched MLP in one seed; the VQC was evaded on 90--100\%, and every learned detector on every
exfiltration it detected.
Single-axis hardening closed held-out jitter evasion to at most 3 of 9 beacons for the MLPs and to 0 of 9
for the tree ensembles, and payload padding then evaded every hardened learned detector on every
detected beacon, in every seed. On exfiltration, the hardened Random Forest let through 0 to 2 held-out
transfers and XGBoost 1 in every seed, and chunk jitter and 8192-byte chunks evaded both on 6 of 6 in
every seed. Evasions tuned against the native MLP transferred to the Random Forest on 16 to 20 of 20 and
to XGBoost on 26 to 30 of 30.

The quantum comparison does depend on it. The VQC had the lowest clean accuracy of the five learned
detectors in 9 of 10 C2 seeds and in all 10 exfiltration seeds. Pooled over seeds, the VQC needed less
jitter than the matched MLP on 157 of 249 paired C2 instances, more on 49, and the same on 43. On
exfiltration it needed a lower throttle on 119 of 257, a higher one on 48, and the same on 90. Counted
per seed, it was the easier target on most paired instances in 8 of 10 seeds on each family. Seed 0 is
an outlier in opposite directions: the most even C2 seed (9 harder, 8 easier) and the most one-sided
exfiltration seed (21 of 21 easier). Transfer between the two varies as much: evasions tuned against
the VQC fooled the native MLP on 1 of 19 to 25 of 30 instances, depending on the seed, so the asymmetry
in Table~\ref{tab:transfer} is not a property of the models.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Dependence on attacker-set flow features}
A neural network, a quantum circuit, two tree ensembles and a one-feature threshold failed in the same
way. What they share is the input: each separates attack from benign mostly through traffic shape the
attacker sets, timing regularity for C2 and throughput for exfiltration. On exfiltration a single cut on
\texttt{bytes\_per\_second} comes within 0.9 points of the best learned detector on clean traffic, so
the learned decision reduces to the one quantity throttling changes. C2 detection uses more than one
feature, since the \texttt{cv\_iat} rule alone flags 52\% of benign flows, and one attacker-controlled
parameter still defeats it.

Tightening the threshold buys detection with false positives (Section~\ref{sec:op}). Stopping jittered
beacons cost the tree ensembles about 5 points of benign FPR, but stopping beacons that also pad their
payload cost the Random Forest a 34\% FPR, and stopping throttled exfiltration cost the MLPs, XGBoost and
the rule 44--49\%. The cost is high because benign traffic has the same shape. In the public capture, about one benign flow in eight is as regular as a beacon. Where hardening pushed
the decision boundary into that region, false positives rose: the VQC's from 0.17 to 0.25 on C2 and
to 0.27 after joint hardening, the \texttt{cv\_iat} rule's to 0.67 after joint hardening, and the
throughput rule's from 0.16 to 0.27 on exfiltration. Against an attacker who adapts, a detector
needs signals the attacker cannot change without breaking the attack or paying for it, such as
destination reputation, behaviour aggregated over many windows, or host telemetry. We did not evaluate
such signals.

\subsection{Quantum robustness claims and problem-space perturbations}
Across ten training seeds the VQC was the weaker detector on both families. Its median clean accuracy
was 80.2\% on C2 and 84.7\% on exfiltration, against 83.6--86.2\% and 87.5--88.4\% for the classical
learned detectors, and on identical flows it was evaded at the lower perturbation in 8 of 10 seeds on
each family. One seed would have told a different story. Seed 0 put the VQC level with the Random
Forest on C2 accuracy and level with the matched MLP on paired C2 evasion (9 against 8), which is where
an earlier draft of this paper concluded there was no consistent difference. West et al.~\cite{west2023nature} measure robustness to small,
$L_p$-bounded perturbations of the encoded input. A problem-space attacker is not limited to small
feature-space changes: raising check-in jitter from 0 to 0.3, which costs the beacon nothing, moves
\texttt{cv\_iat} from near 0 into the range of benign traffic. A model of any class that relies on
\texttt{cv\_iat} will change its verdict under a shift that large. At seed 0 the VQC reached 74.6\% on
a dataset one third the size and 84.1\% on the full one. A comparison of evasion thresholds on the
smaller dataset would have mixed the effect of the architecture with the effect of undertraining. Model
families should be compared where clean detection is neither saturated nor short of data, and over
several training seeds.

\subsection{Adversarial training and untrained attack parameters}
Hardening against timing jitter cut held-out jitter evasion from 100\% to 0\% for the MLPs and tree
ensembles, and payload-size padding then evaded every hardened C2 detector in a median of two to six
queries. Exfiltration showed what the hardened models had learned. After hardening on throttled
exfiltration, Random Forest and XGBoost stopped throttled transfers even at rates inside the benign
trickle band, and their feature importances had moved to packet-size and volume features, the
parameters our sweep held fixed. New volumes alone evaded the hardened XGBoost, adding chunk-timing
jitter evaded both ensembles, and 8192-byte chunks brought every hardened learned detector back to
within 2.8\,s of its unhardened evasion cost (1.1--1.6\,s for all but the matched MLP). Adversarial
training on one attack configuration taught these models that configuration, and the attacker controls
parameters the training data never varied.

Covering two parameters at once did not fix this for C2. Joint timing+size hardening held the tree
ensembles to at most 1 of 9 evasions along each of the two lines it was trained on, and raised the
padding the MLPs and VQC needed from $0.1$--$0.3$ to $0.4$--$0.6$ at seed 0. A beacon at timing jitter
$0.3$, between the trained points, then evaded the jointly hardened MLPs and VQC on 6 to 9 of 9 held-out
instances in every training seed, and XGBoost on 2 to 7. Only the Random Forest held, at 2 of 9 in every
seed and a benign FPR of 0.170 at seed 0, below its undefended 0.185. Across seeds, joint hardening raised
the benign FPR over single-axis hardening in most seeds for four of the five learned detectors, by a
median of 0.7 to 2.2 points; XGBoost was the exception. The training data covered two lines through a
two-parameter space. XGBoost held on both lines and missed part of the region between them; the MLPs
and the VQC did not hold even on held-out beacons of the size line in most seeds.

Training on random points across the whole region, with the same number of added beacons, closed the
region for both tree ensembles: at most 1 of 9 held-out instances inside it evaded either, in every
seed, at a median benign FPR within 0.8 points of joint hardening. It did not extend past the region.
Size jitter above the trained maximum of $0.6$ evaded the range-wide XGBoost on 8 or 9 of 9 held-out
instances in every seed and the Random Forest on 3 to 5, and beacons past both edges evaded both at
seed 0 on 20 and 22 of 28 points. Every one of those beacons still worked. A defender who hardens over a
parameter range has to choose its upper end, and the attacker pays nothing to go past it. Our threat
model gives the beacon four such parameters (Section~\ref{sec:threat}), and we hardened against two.

\subsection{Matching query conditions to training captures}
Our first exfiltration sweep ran evasion queries in a two-host network without background traffic. The
naive exfiltration flows it produced were faster and more regular than any training exfiltration, and
two detectors with test recall of 0.94 or higher called most of them benign. That is an evasion at zero
perturbation, caused by the query conditions. Running queries inside the training scenario, with its
background traffic, removed it. A problem-space evaluation generates its own test traffic, and the
unperturbed attack must be in-distribution for the detector, or the measured evasion mixes the attack
with a train/test mismatch. Reporting the naive detection rate next to every evasion rate exposes such
a mismatch, because the naive rate drops.

\subsection{The observation window}
\label{sec:window}
Our features are computed over a 60\,s window, while operational beacon hunting often scores a
destination over hours or days of connection logs~\cite{gardiner2014}. A reviewer may ask whether a
longer window would recover the signal. For the features we attack, it would not. The evasion raises
the beacon's actual check-in jitter, so the inter-arrival times are drawn from a wider distribution for
as long as the beacon runs. A longer window estimates the same \texttt{cv\_iat} more precisely, and
that value now sits in the benign range; more samples of a benign-range statistic do not make it
malicious. The 60\,s window already holds 7 to 30 check-ins per beacon, enough to estimate
\texttt{cv\_iat}, so the evasion is not an artifact of a short, noisy estimate. What a longer horizon
buys the defender is different features, not a better estimate of these: the count of distinct
destinations a host contacts over a day, the rarity of a destination, or the total bytes a host ships
over a week. Those are not attacker-set per-flow shape, and we did not evaluate them. The window is a
limit on which features we tested, not on whether the tested features survive more observation.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Emulated testbed and synthetic benign traffic.} All traffic is generated in Mininet, a
        lab-only evaluation in the sense of Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022}. Our
        benign mix is about $3.4\times$ denser in the beacon timing band than the real reference host
        (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}), so our absolute false-positive rates are higher than a deployment
        on that host's traffic would see. The evasion results do not depend on this density.
  \item \emph{Small held-out sets.} Hardening, single-axis, joint and range-wide, is evaluated on 9 held-out
        instances per family and each exfiltration escalation sweep on 6, so a 6/6 result has a Wilson
        lower bound of 0.61. These sweeps test a specific explanation and do not estimate rates
        precisely.
  \item \emph{Training seeds vary the model, not the data.} The ten-seed study changes initialisation
        and sampling with the split and the captured traffic held fixed. We did not recapture the training
        traffic, so variation from a different set of scenario runs is not measured.
  \item \emph{Finite grids.} The main sweeps take timing jitter to $0.8$, size jitter to $0.6$ and the
        throttle to $c=12$; the outside-range C2 sweeps take timing jitter to $2.0$ and size jitter to
        $1.0$. A detector that is not evaded on a grid is resistant only up to its top value.
  \item \emph{Two attack families, binary tasks.} Both tasks separate one attack class from benign
        traffic. In attacks whose signal is bound to their function, such as volumetric denial of
        service, reducing the signal also reduces the attack's effect, and our results do not cover
        them.
  \item \emph{Simulated, single-architecture VQC.} The VQC runs on a noiseless state-vector simulator
        with one circuit design (8 qubits, 4 entangling layers); other encodings or hardware noise
        could behave differently.
  \item \emph{Weak attacker.} The attacker is a black-box search along one axis at a time or over
        random points, with no model knowledge, so an adaptive attacker would do at least as well.
  \item \emph{Single observation window.} Features are computed over a fixed 60\,s window. Detectors
        that aggregate different features over longer horizons were not evaluated; the per-flow shape
        features we did test do not recover under more observation, for the reason given above.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We evaluated flow-level intrusion detectors against problem-space evasion, in which every perturbation
is sent as real traffic and admitted only if the attack keeps working. Across C2 beaconing and data
exfiltration, six classical, tree-based, rule-based and quantum detectors were evaded on 326 of the
327 cases in which a detector caught the unmodified attack. The median C2 evasion needed at most
$\pm30\%$ check-in jitter and none needed more than $\pm80\%$; exfiltration evasion added a median of at
most 2.7\,s to a 3.3\,s transfer, against a 60\,s deadline. Over ten training seeds, the VQC had lower clean
accuracy than the MLP on its inputs in every run and was the easier target in 8 of 10 seeds on each
family; a single seed would have shown it level on C2. Adversarial training closed the attacked axis for the trained configuration only, and
changing one further parameter (payload size for C2, volume, chunk jitter or chunk size for
exfiltration) restored evasion at close to the original cost. Hardening on timing and size together
held against the combined attack only for the Random Forest, in every seed. Hardening on random points
across the whole timing and size range held both tree ensembles inside that range and failed again
just outside it: padding past the trained maximum evaded the range-wide XGBoost on 8 or 9 of 9 held-out
beacons in every seed. All six detectors depend on flow features
the attacker sets. Two questions remain open: how attacks whose signal is bound to their function
behave under the same evaluation, and whether features an attacker cannot change cheaply resist this
kind of evasion.
