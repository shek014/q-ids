\begin{abstract}
Adversarial evasion of machine-learning network intrusion detectors is mostly demonstrated in
\emph{feature space}, by perturbing flow feature vectors that may correspond to no traffic an attacker
could send. We instead measure \emph{problem-space} evasion: every candidate perturbation is generated
as real traffic in an emulated network, re-featurized, and admitted only if a functional oracle confirms
the attack still works. We study two attack families whose detectable signal is a parameter the attacker
sets---command-and-control (C2) beaconing (timing regularity) and data exfiltration (throughput)---and
six detectors: a from-scratch neural network, an 8-qubit variational quantum classifier trained on
matched inputs, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a single-feature rule. Every detector is evaded for
essentially every attack instance it detected: C2 beacons by randomizing check-in timing by about
$\pm$20--30\%, exfiltration by slowing the transfer two- to threefold, from about 3\,s to 6\,s against a
60\,s deadline. The quantum classifier shows no consistent robustness advantage. Adversarial training
closes the axis it is trained on, but only for the configuration it was shown: payload-size padding
re-breaks every hardened C2 detector, and changing one further transfer parameter (volume, chunk timing
or chunk size) restores exfiltration evasion against hardened detectors that had resisted throttling. A
check against a public capture shows that real benign flows also occupy the beacon timing region,
though less densely than our synthetic mix. Flow-level detectors that rely on attacker-controlled
traffic shape are fragile under realizable evasion, and single-axis hardening does not repair them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) are a mature research topic, and so is
their vulnerability to adversarial evasion~\cite{nids_survey2023,ennaji2024,nids_understanding2026}.
Most of that vulnerability, however, has been shown in \emph{feature space}: the attacker perturbs a
flow's feature vector directly, often with gradient access, and success is measured on the perturbed
vector. A perturbed feature vector is not traffic. It may correspond to no sequence of packets an
attacker could send, or to traffic that no longer performs the attack. This gap has drawn sustained
criticism: Apruzzese et al.~\cite{apruzzese2022} argue for threat models with realistic attacker
knowledge and control, and elShehaby and Matrawy~\cite{elshehaby2023} contend that evasion against real
ML-NIDS remains largely impractical. Whether an attacker can evade a trained detector with traffic it
can actually send, and at what cost, remains an open practical question.

The \emph{problem-space} setting of Pierazzi et al.~\cite{pierazzi2020} makes the question precise: the
attacker must produce a real object, and perturbations are limited to transformations that are
realizable and preserve the object's function. For network traffic this is hard to evaluate. The map
from packets to flow features is neither invertible nor differentiable, and static benchmark datasets
cannot be regenerated with different attacker parameters. Evaluating problem-space evasion needs a
traffic generator in the loop: each candidate perturbation must be sent, captured and re-featurized
before the detector sees it, and its effect on the attack itself must be checked.

We evaluate this setting on two attack families chosen because their detectable signal is set by the
attacker. A command-and-control (C2) beacon checks in with its server at a regular interval, and
flow-level beacon detectors key on exactly that regularity~\cite{yilmaz2026,gardiner2014}; but within the
latency an operator tolerates, randomizing the interval or padding the payload costs the beacon nothing.
Data exfiltration is a deliberate contrast: its signal is throughput, and slowing the transfer to evade
a rate-based detector has a real cost, because the data must still leave before a deadline. In both
cases, benign networks contain traffic with the same shape---periodic NTP and health checks, bulk
backups and downloads---which bounds how strictly a detector can treat that shape as malicious.

A second question concerns the detector. A growing literature applies variational and other
quantum-classical classifiers to intrusion detection and reports clean accuracy competitive with
classical baselines~\cite{abreu2024,thomos2026,qvcnn2024}, while a separate line asks whether quantum
classifiers are inherently more robust to adversarial perturbation~\cite{west2023nature,west2023bench}.
Both evaluate on static features or with $L_p$-bounded feature-space perturbations, so neither says
whether a quantum detector is harder to evade with real traffic. We therefore train a classical
multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a variational quantum classifier (VQC) on identical reduced inputs, and
place both against the detectors a defender would actually deploy: Random Forest, XGBoost, and a
single-feature statistical rule.

All traffic in our evaluation is generated in an emulated Mininet network. A model-in-the-loop sweep
sends real attacks with candidate parameters, extracts their flow features, and scores them against
every detector offline; a functional oracle admits a perturbation only if the beacon keeps completing
check-ins within a 30\,s latency budget, or the exfiltrated payload is confirmed delivered at the
collector within a 60\,s deadline. We then harden every detector by adversarial training, evaluate it
on held-out attack instances, and test whether the attacker can recover by changing a parameter the
hardening did not cover.

The results are uniformly unfavourable to the defender. Every detector is evaded for essentially every
attack instance it detected, at a cost of about $\pm$20--30\% check-in jitter for C2 and a two- to
threefold slowdown for exfiltration---a few seconds against a 60\,s deadline. The tree ensembles are
no harder to evade than the MLP, and the quantum classifier shows no consistent robustness advantage.
Adversarial training closes the axis it is trained on, but for the trained configuration only:
payload-size padding re-breaks every hardened C2 detector, and hardened tree ensembles that resisted
exfiltration throttling are evaded again as soon as the attacker also changes the transfer volume,
chunk timing or chunk size.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A model-in-the-loop, functionality-preserving problem-space evaluation of flow-level NIDS for
        two attack families, in which every perturbation is realized as real traffic and checked by a
        functional oracle, with evasion counted only against attacks the detector originally caught.
  \item A comparison of six detectors---a from-scratch MLP, a VQC on matched inputs, Random Forest,
        XGBoost and a single-feature rule---showing that all are evaded at low realizable cost and that,
        to our knowledge in the first problem-space comparison of this kind, the quantum classifier
        offers no consistent robustness advantage.
  \item Evidence, across both attack families, that adversarial training defends the attack
        \emph{configuration} it was trained on rather than the attack: changing one further
        attacker-controlled parameter restores evasion at close to the original cost.
  \item A methodological finding that evasion queries must reproduce the network conditions of the
        training captures: run in an uncontended topology, exfiltration queries were out of
        distribution and produced spurious ``evasions''.
  \item An open, reproducible testbed---traffic generators, functional oracles, detectors, sweeps and
        captured data---and a check of the synthetic benign mix against a public real-traffic capture.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work,
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the threat model, testbed, detectors and evasion procedure,
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their implications
and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work sits at the intersection of four strands: problem-space (realizable) adversarial machine
learning, adversarial evasion against network intrusion detection (NIDS), machine-learning detection
of C2 beaconing, and quantum machine learning for security together with its (separately studied)
adversarial robustness.

\subsection{Problem-space vs.\ feature-space adversarial attacks}
Most adversarial-ML results perturb the \emph{feature vector} directly and measure success in feature
space. Pierazzi et al.~\cite{pierazzi2020} formalized the alternative \emph{problem-space} setting, in
which the attacker must produce a real object (there, an Android app) and the perturbation is
constrained by what is physically realizable---available transformations, preserved semantics, absent
artifacts, and plausibility---because in most domains there is no clean inverse map from a desired
feature vector back to a valid object. Chernikova and Oprea's FENCE~\cite{fence2022} pushed in the
same direction for deep models in \emph{constrained} domains, generating evasions that respect feature
inter-dependencies, with network security among the case studies. These works establish the discipline
we adopt---realizability and functionality preservation as first-class constraints---but for malware
and engineered-feature settings. We carry it into \emph{network flow} NIDS with an in-the-loop traffic
generator: every candidate perturbation is realized as actual packets and admitted only if a functional
oracle confirms the attack still works (Section~\ref{sec:evasion}).

\subsection{Adversarial evasion against NIDS}
Adversarial attacks on ML-based NIDS are by now a large
literature~\cite{nids_survey2023,ennaji2024,nids_understanding2026}. A recurring critique is that many
reported successes are demonstrated in
feature space or under idealized threat models an attacker could not realize on the wire. Apruzzese et
al.~\cite{apruzzese2022} argued for realistic threat models with limited attacker knowledge and
control, and elShehaby and Matrawy~\cite{elshehaby2023} went further, contending that evasion attacks
\emph{remain largely impractical} against real ML-NIDS, especially dynamic ones. A 2024 line of work
begins to close this gap with explicitly problem-space NIDS attacks~\cite{catillo2024}, and related
efforts restrict perturbations to attacker-controllable fields: the controllability-aware framework
of~\cite{li2026} partitions flow features into directly-, indirectly-, and un-controllable groups and
perturbs only the controllable ones, while payload-padding attacks~\cite{jing2025} evade classifiers by
adding bytes that do not disturb transmission.

Our work is a direct response to the realism critique, and sharpens rather than contradicts it. We do
not claim a generic realizable evasion; we isolate one attack class---C2 beaconing---where the
detectable signal (timing/size regularity) is \emph{exactly} a set of parameters the attacker already
controls for free, and show that there the evasion is not only practical but succeeds for 100\% of
instances while a functional oracle confirms the beacon keeps working. The controllable-field idea
of~\cite{li2026} appears here as the physical timing-jitter and size-jitter axes; unlike
feature-masking frameworks, our controllability is grounded in a generator that must emit valid
traffic. This also lets us measure quantities a feature-space attack cannot define honestly: minimal
\emph{realizable} evading perturbation, queries-to-evasion against a black-box verdict, and functional
admissibility.

\subsection{Detecting C2 beaconing}
Flow-level detection of command-and-control beaconing keys on \emph{timing regularity}: a beacon checks
in at a near-constant interval, and the standard statistical signal is a low coefficient of variation
(CV) of inter-arrival times, often combined with anomaly detectors~\cite{yilmaz2026}, with surveys of
C2 technique and defence going back a decade~\cite{gardiner2014}. This is the detection premise we
adopt (our \texttt{cv\_iat} feature is precisely this CV; Section~\ref{sec:features})---and precisely
the premise we attack. The beaconing-detection literature treats timing regularity as a reliable
discriminator; we show that because regularity is an attacker-set parameter with functional slack, a
detector that relies on it is defeated by a cost-free increase in jitter, and that the near-identical
\emph{rigid benign} traffic (NTP, health checks, keepalives) bounds how aggressively any such detector
can tighten without false alarms.

\subsection{Quantum machine learning for intrusion detection}
A growing body of work applies quantum and quantum-classical models---variational quantum classifiers
(VQCs), quantum SVMs, quantum CNNs---to intrusion detection, generally reporting accuracy competitive
with or exceeding classical baselines~\cite{abreu2024,thomos2026,qvcnn2024}, within a broader
QML-for-security literature~\cite{qml_sec_taxonomy2025}. This literature is almost entirely about
\emph{clean} accuracy; it does not evaluate robustness to realizable evasion, and typically works from
static feature datasets. We add the missing axis: a VQC and a classical MLP trained on \emph{matched
inputs} (Section~\ref{sec:detectors}) and compared not on clean accuracy alone but on problem-space
robustness to the same realized attack traffic.

\subsection{Adversarial robustness of quantum classifiers}
Separately from the IDS setting, a quantum-ML-theory literature asks whether quantum classifiers are
\emph{inherently} more robust. Early results showed VQCs are, like classical networks, vulnerable to
adversarial examples~\cite{lu2020,liao2021}, but subsequent work raised the possibility of a
\emph{quantum robustness advantage}---most prominently West et al.~\cite{west2023nature}, who report
that quantum models can learn features classical attacks do not disturb, with scaling
studies~\cite{west2023bench} and a recent scoping review~\cite{qml_robust_review2026} mapping the area.
Crucially, this literature evaluates robustness in \emph{feature space} ($L_p$-bounded perturbations of
the encoded input), where ``robustness'' need not correspond to anything an attacker could realize.

Our contribution to this strand is, to our knowledge, the first \emph{problem-space} robustness
comparison of a quantum vs.\ classical classifier on a security task: robustness measured as resistance
to realized, functionality-preserving attack \emph{traffic} rather than to $L_p$ feature perturbations.
We find no quantum robustness advantage---the VQC is, if anything, marginally easier to evade on the
realistic task, and an apparent edge on a saturated (100\%-clean-accuracy) version of the task
disappears once detection is made realistic (Section~\ref{sec:paired}).

\subsection{Adversarial training and its limits}
Adversarial training is the standard defense and is studied for NIDS~\cite{ennaji2024,nids_advtrain2026},
but it is known to overfit to the perturbation \emph{type} used during training and to generalize
poorly to unseen or transfer attacks. We instantiate this limitation concretely as a two-axis arms
race: hardening against the timing-jitter attack drives jitter evasion to zero (at a measurable
false-positive cost) but leaves the previously-unused payload-size axis open, and the hardened detector
is re-broken for 100\% of instances by size padding alone (Section~\ref{sec:defense}). Because
realizable traffic offers several such functionally-free axes, single-axis adversarial training
\emph{relocates} the vulnerability rather than closing it.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

We evaluate the robustness of flow-level machine-learning intrusion detectors to \emph{realizable}
(problem-space) adversarial evasion, and compare a classical neural network against a
quantum-classical classifier under identical conditions. All code and captured data are released for
reproducibility (Section~Data availability).

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}
We consider an attacker who has compromised an internal host and operates a command-and-control (C2)
beacon: the host periodically ``checks in'' to an attacker-controlled server to receive commands. A
NIDS observes traffic at the flow level and classifies each flow as benign or C2. The attacker's goal
is to evade detection while \emph{preserving functionality}---the beacon must keep maintaining its
control channel. The attacker:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{can} manipulate the realizable parameters of their own traffic: the check-in interval,
        the timing jitter (randomness of the interval), the payload size, and the size jitter. These
        are degrees of freedom a real C2 implementation controls and that do not, within bounds,
        impair the beacon;
  \item \textbf{cannot} alter other hosts' traffic, the detector's parameters, or the
        feature-extraction pipeline, and cannot submit feature vectors directly---every change must be
        realized as actual traffic on the wire;
  \item \textbf{can} query the detector in a black-box manner (observe benign/malicious verdicts),
        modelling an attacker who tests candidate configurations against a deployed or surrogate
        detector.
\end{itemize}
This is a \emph{problem-space} threat model~\cite{pierazzi2020}: perturbations are constrained to what
is physically realizable and functionality-preserving, in contrast to feature-space attacks that
perturb the feature vector directly and may correspond to no realizable traffic. A perturbation is
\emph{admissible} only if the resulting beacon remains functional (Section~\ref{sec:evasion}).

\subsection{Testbed and traffic generation}
\label{sec:testbed}
Problem-space evasion requires \emph{controllable} traffic generation---static benchmark datasets
cannot be manipulated in the loop---so we emulate a network in Mininet~\cite{mininet2010}. Hosts attach
to a single Open vSwitch switch via \texttt{TCLink} links capped at 10\,Mbit/s, which bounds capture
volume to a realistic segment rate. On every host we disable NIC offloads (\texttt{gso}, \texttt{tso},
\texttt{gro}, \texttt{lro}) so the capture records real MTU-sized packets rather than offload
super-segments, and we pre-populate ARP tables (\texttt{staticArp}) so address resolution does not
appear as stray flows. Traffic is captured with \texttt{tcpdump} inside the relevant host's network
namespace at a 128-byte snap length (headers only); per-packet size features use the recorded wire
length rather than the truncated capture length.

Each scenario runs for 60\,s and is repeated with per-host, per-run randomized parameters to produce a
dataset. One host (h1) acts as the victim/collector and runs the services; the remaining hosts generate
traffic in one of the roles below.

\paragraph{C2 beacons (malicious).} A beacon sends a single UDP datagram check-in to a C2 endpoint
every \texttt{interval} seconds ($\pm$\,\texttt{jitter}), with payload size \texttt{payload\_size}
($\pm$\,\texttt{size\_jitter}), and reads back a reply. Modelling check-ins as single datagrams
(DNS/heartbeat-style C2) means a flow's packet inter-arrival times \emph{are} its check-in intervals,
so timing regularity is expressed directly in the flow features rather than being obscured by
connection-level mechanics. A naive beacon is rigid: low \texttt{jitter} and low \texttt{size\_jitter}.

\paragraph{Benign traffic.} Crucially for a realistic detection task, benign traffic spans the full
range of \emph{regularity}. Real networks contain highly regular benign traffic---NTP, health checks,
keepalives, telemetry---nearly indistinguishable from a beacon at the flow level, which is precisely
why flow-based C2 detection is hard. We generate: (a) \emph{rigid} benign periodic traffic in the
\emph{same} timing/size band as the beacons (NTP/strict-health-check analogues); (b) moderately regular
keepalives; (c) loose, irregular polling; (d) bursty short TCP application sessions across several
ports, some failing; and (e) bulk transfer. The rigid benign traffic~(a) is a deliberate confuser: the
detector cannot perfectly separate it from a beacon, which keeps clean accuracy realistic rather than
saturated (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The beacons and periodic benign traffic (a--c) come from the
\emph{same} generator differing only in the jitter/size regime; the role (hence label) is assigned per
host. The contested boundary is therefore near-boundary by construction (Table~\ref{tab:scenario}).

\paragraph{Realism check.} To test whether real benign traffic shows the timing regularity our
confuser assumes, we ran a public capture of a normal host~\cite{ctunormal29} through the same pipeline:
the host's outbound packets over two hours, cut into 60\,s windows to match the scenario window
(7{,}456 flows with at least five packets). Real benign flows do occupy the beacon region: 12.3\% are
as regular as our C2 beacons ($\texttt{cv\_iat} \le 0.111$, the 95th percentile of the C2 class;
Fig.~\ref{fig:realism}). Our synthetic benign mix is, however, about $3.4\times$ denser there (41.9\%),
so it is a deliberately hard setting: the clean false-positive rates we report likely overstate those of
a deployment on traffic like this host's. Two qualifications apply. The reference host only browses the
web, with no NTP, health checks or monitoring agents, so an enterprise segment plausibly contains
\emph{more} regular benign traffic than it does; and part of the real low-\texttt{cv\_iat} mass comes from
evenly paced bulk transfers rather than periodic check-ins---which a timing-regularity feature cannot
tell apart either. The evasion results do not depend on this density: evasion moves the beacon into the
benign region however densely it is populated.

\subsection{Flow features and labeling}
\label{sec:features}
Packets are aggregated into flows keyed by the tuple (protocol, source MAC, destination) rather than
the 5-tuple. The 5-tuple is unsuitable here because an attacker can vary source ports and spoof
addresses, fragmenting one logical activity into many single-packet ``flows''; keying on the sender's
hardware address---which the generators never spoof---collapses each host's activity into one flow whose
aggregate statistics are meaningful, and makes port diversity a \emph{within-flow} signal. Each flow is
labeled by the role of its source MAC; the collector/victim host's own traffic is excluded.

From each flow we extract 17 flow-level features: \texttt{duration}, \texttt{packet\_count},
\texttt{byte\_count}, \texttt{mean\_packet\_size}, \texttt{std\_packet\_size},
\texttt{packets\_per\_second}, \texttt{bytes\_per\_second}, the TCP flag counts
\texttt{syn/ack/fin/rst\_count}, \texttt{mean\_iat}, \texttt{std\_iat}, \texttt{cv\_iat},
\texttt{unique\_dst\_ports}, \texttt{unique\_src\_ports}, and a categorical \texttt{protocol}. Here
\texttt{iat} denotes inter-arrival time and $\texttt{cv\_iat} = \texttt{std\_iat}/\texttt{mean\_iat}$ is
the coefficient of variation of inter-arrival times---a scale-invariant measure of timing regularity.
\texttt{cv\_iat} is central to this study: a rigid beacon has low \texttt{cv\_iat} at any interval,
whereas naturally-jittered benign traffic has high \texttt{cv\_iat}, and (unlike raw \texttt{std\_iat})
it does not conflate regularity with interval length.

Formally, each flow is summarized by a feature vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$
(Table~\ref{tab:features}). Writing $\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_m$ for a flow's inter-arrival times with mean
$\mu_{\mathrm{IAT}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{IAT}}$, the timing-regularity feature is
their coefficient of variation,
\begin{equation}
\texttt{cv\_iat} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IAT}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{IAT}}},
\label{eq:cviat}
\end{equation}
which is scale-invariant: a rigid beacon has $\texttt{cv\_iat}\approx 0$ at any interval, whereas
naturally-jittered benign traffic has $\texttt{cv\_iat}=\mathcal{O}(1)$.

\subsection{Detectors and the matched-input protocol}
\label{sec:detectors}
We compare two detectors on an identical binary task (benign vs.\ C2), and place them against three
reference detectors representative of deployed practice (end of this subsection).

\paragraph{Classical MLP.} A multilayer perceptron implemented from scratch in NumPy (no ML
frameworks): two hidden layers of 32 and 16 units with ReLU activations and a 2-way softmax output,
trained by mini-batch gradient descent with Adam (learning rate 0.01, batch size 64, up to 150 epochs
with early stopping, patience 20) under class-balanced loss weighting. The softmax + cross-entropy
gradient was verified against finite differences.

\paragraph{Quantum-classical VQC.} A variational quantum classifier implemented in
PennyLane~\cite{pennylane2018}: an \texttt{AngleEmbedding} of the (PCA-reduced) features onto 8 qubits,
followed by 4 \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers}, reading out one Pauli-$Z$ expectation per class from
the first two wires, passed through a softmax and trained with cross-entropy (learning rate 0.05, batch
size 16, up to 30 epochs, patience 8). Training uses exact gradients via the \texttt{default.qubit}
state-vector simulator.
Concretely, the PCA features $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}$ are angle-encoded as single-qubit $R_Y$
rotations $U(\mathbf{z})$, a trainable \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers} block $V(\boldsymbol{\Theta})$
is applied, and one Pauli-$Z$ expectation is read from each of the first two wires,
\begin{equation}
f_i(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\Theta}) \;=\;
\langle 0|\,U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{z})\,V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\,Z_i\,V(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\,U(\mathbf{z})\,|0\rangle,
\qquad i\in\{0,1\},
\label{eq:vqc}
\end{equation}
yielding class probabilities $P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})=\mathrm{softmax}\big(2\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\Theta})\big)$.
The circuit has $4\times 8\times 3 = 96$ trainable parameters.

\paragraph{Matched inputs.} To make the comparison attributable to the model rather than the
information it receives, both detectors are trained on \emph{identical} inputs for the comparison:
features are standardized (z-score, fit on the training split only) and reduced by PCA to the
8-component qubit budget; for the VQC the components are additionally rescaled into the angle range
required by \texttt{AngleEmbedding}, a monotone per-feature transform carrying no additional
information. We also train the MLP on the full 17-dimensional (``native'') feature set to characterize
the detector a defender would actually deploy. All preprocessing transforms are fit on the training
split only. The dataset comprises 1{,}260 flows (900 benign, 360 C2) from 180 scenario runs, split
70/15/15 into train/validation/test, stratified by class.

Both detectors share the same preprocessing up to the model: raw features are standardized using
training-split statistics and, in the matched regime, projected onto their first eight principal
components,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} \;=\; \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{PCA}}^{\top}\big((\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})/\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{8},
\label{eq:pca}
\end{equation}
which retain 99.6\% of the variance (the division is per-feature); for the VQC each component is then
affinely mapped into the $R_Y$ angle range. A detector outputs a posterior $P(\text{benign}\mid\cdot)$
and flags a flow as malicious when
\begin{equation}
P(\text{benign}\mid\cdot) < \tau, \qquad \tau = 0.5 .
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:detectors} summarizes the two detectors and Table~\ref{tab:dataset} the dataset split.

\paragraph{Reference detectors.} To place the MLP--VQC comparison against standard practice, we also
train three reference detectors on the same split and the native 17 features: a Random Forest (300
trees, class-balanced), gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, depth 4, early-stopped on the validation
split), and a single-feature statistical rule---the best single cut on \texttt{cv\_iat} for C2 (on
\texttt{bytes\_per\_second} for exfiltration), chosen on the training split to maximize balanced
accuracy. Unlike the MLP and VQC, these use library implementations (scikit-learn, XGBoost): their role
is to show what a defender would deploy, not to be compared on equal footing. All are scored through
the same decision rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:decision}).

\subsection{Problem-space evasion}
\label{sec:evasion}
The evasion is a model-in-the-loop search for the smallest realizable, functionality-preserving
perturbation that flips the detector's verdict to benign.

\paragraph{Query primitive.} A single query takes a parameter vector
\[
\theta = (\texttt{interval},\ \texttt{jitter},\ \texttt{payload\_size},\ \texttt{size\_jitter}),
\]
generates a \emph{real} beacon with
those parameters in a short Mininet capture, extracts the beacon's flow, and returns its 17-feature
vector. The capture window matches the 60\,s training window. C2 queries run in a minimal two-host
topology without background traffic; query beacons are therefore somewhat \emph{more} regular than
training beacons (which pick up queuing noise from the shared link), i.e.\ easier to detect, so the
evasion thresholds we report for C2 are conservative (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Functional oracle.} During each query the beacon logs the timestamp of every \emph{successful}
(round-trip-confirmed) check-in. The beacon is deemed \emph{functional} iff it completed at least two
successful check-ins and no silent period---including the stretches before the first and after the
last check-in---exceeded a maximum tolerable control-latency $T_{\max}=30$\,s
(\texttt{MAX\_INTERVAL}). Writing $t_1<\dots<t_C$ for the timestamps of the successful check-ins and
$t_0$, $t_{C+1}$ for the start and end of the capture window,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Func}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,C \ge 2 \;\wedge\; \max_{0\le k\le C}(t_{k+1}-t_k) \le T_{\max}\,\right].
\label{eq:oracle}
\end{equation}
Including the window edges catches a beacon that falls silent partway through the capture. This
operationalizes ``the attack still works'': a
perturbation that silences the beacon or stretches its gaps past the operator's tolerance is
inadmissible.

\paragraph{Line search.} For a given detector and a given realizable axis, we fix the other parameters
and sweep the axis over an ascending grid. Let $\Phi(\theta)$ denote generating a beacon with parameters $\theta$ as real traffic and extracting
its 17-feature vector, and let $\theta\oplus\delta\mathbf{e}_a$ increment realizable axis $a$ by
$\delta$. For each beacon instance (seed) the minimal evading perturbation is
\begin{equation}
\delta^{\star} \;=\; \min\big\{\,\delta\ge 0 \;:\;
P(\text{benign}\mid\Phi(\theta\oplus\delta\mathbf{e}_a)) \ge \tau \;\wedge\; \mathrm{Func}(\theta\oplus\delta\mathbf{e}_a)=1\,\big\},
\label{eq:evasion}
\end{equation}
the smallest functionality-preserving move along $a$ that flips the verdict (Eq.~\ref{eq:decision}). We
find it by sweeping the ascending grid and record the queries spent reaching it (queries-to-evasion). We
report evasion success rate, the distribution of $\delta^{\star}$, and queries-to-evasion.

A \emph{beacon instance} is a (configuration, seed) pair: the configuration fixes the operating
point---here the check-in interval, with payload $64$\,B and zero size-jitter---and the seed is an
independent RNG draw within it. We sweep three intervals ($2$, $4$, $8$\,s) with ten seeds each,
$n=30$ instances in all, so a result is reported \emph{across} operating points rather than at a single
one.

\paragraph{Detected-only scoring.} An instance counts toward a detector's evasion rate only if that
detector flagged the \emph{unmodified} attack (the grid floor, e.g.\ jitter $0$). A detector that already
calls the naive attack benign has not been evaded---it failed at detection---so we report its naive
detection rate separately, next to every evasion rate. Every detector is evaded for all detected
instances at the default $\tau=0.5$, so we do not additionally compare detectors at a matched
false-positive rate: no operating point would change a conclusion. Along the jitter axis we report the minimal evading perturbation as a \emph{bracket}
$(j_{-},\,j_{+}]$---the largest grid value that did not evade and the smallest that did---rather than a
single grid point, so a reported figure is a resolved interval, not a one-sided upper bound.
We search the timing-jitter axis first (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and the payload-size-jitter axis for
the defense escalation (Section~\ref{sec:defense}); both are functionally free in the sense that neither
prevents a check-in.

\subsection{Paired transferability}
\label{sec:paired-method}
To compare the two detectors on \emph{identical} traffic and to measure cross-model transfer, we
decouple capture from scoring. A \emph{capture-once sweep} runs the full jitter grid for every beacon
instance a single time, saving each flow's features and functional verdict with no detector in the
loop. These flows are then scored \emph{offline against each detector}, which is the single source of
every evasion number we report: (a) each detector's evasion rate and minimal-evading-jitter
distribution (Section~\ref{sec:jitter}), (b) a \emph{paired} comparison of minimal evading jitter on the
same flows, and (c) transferability: whether a beacon tuned to just evade one detector also evades the
other. Because the captured flows are a property of the
attack and not of any detector, the same sweep is reused to evaluate every (including
adversarially-trained) model, which also removes capture variance from the comparison.

\subsection{Adversarial-training defense and axis escalation}
\label{sec:defense-method}
Finally we test whether adversarial training restores robustness. We fold evaded beacons from the sweep
into the training set labeled as C2 and retrain each detector; to avoid leakage, we split the instances
by configuration, folding a stratified two-thirds into training and holding the rest out for
evaluation (21 training and 9 held-out instances, three per interval). We report the hardened
detectors' clean accuracy and, in particular, their benign
false-positive rate, since teaching the detector that jittered beacons are malicious risks flagging the
near-identical rigid benign traffic. We then evaluate in two steps. First, we re-score the held-out
jitter evasions to test whether the original attack still succeeds. Second---because the jitter defense
can only exploit the timing axis---we mount an \emph{axis escalation}: holding timing jitter at an
already-evading value, we run the line search on the previously-unused payload-size-jitter axis against
the hardened detector. This tests whether a defender who hardens against one realizable axis has closed
the vulnerability or merely displaced it to another equally-free axis.

\subsection{Second attack family: data exfiltration}
\label{sec:exfil-method}
To test whether the problem-space result is specific to C2 beaconing or holds for a structurally
different attack, we add a second family: data exfiltration. It is a deliberate contrast---its
detectable signal is \emph{throughput} rather than timing regularity, and its evasion (throttling)
trades directly against a functional deadline rather than being free. The testbed, flow features,
detectors, and matched-input protocol (Sections~\ref{sec:testbed}--\ref{sec:detectors}) are reused
unchanged; only the attack, its benign confuser, and the evasion axis differ.

\paragraph{Threat model.} The attacker has staged a fixed body of data on a compromised internal host
and must move it to an external collector; the NIDS classifies each flow as benign or exfiltration.
The attacker \textbf{can} manipulate the realizable transfer parameters---rate, chunking, and
inter-chunk timing---but \textbf{cannot} reduce the total volume that must leave (the payload is fixed),
nor alter other hosts' traffic or the detector. A perturbation is admissible only if the full payload
is delivered within an operational deadline (the functional oracle below).

\paragraph{Traffic and benign confuser.} A single generator produces exfiltration as one TCP transfer
of a fixed \texttt{volume} to a collector, paced to a target rate by inter-chunk delays; a \emph{naive}
exfil dumps the payload as fast as it can (a short, high-throughput flow). As for C2, the benign mix is
enriched so detection is realistic rather than saturated. The decisive confuser is \textbf{high-rate
benign bulk}---backups, large downloads, database replication---drawn from the \emph{same} volume and
rate bands as the naive exfil, so a flow-level detector cannot separate the two without false-flagging
benign bulk. (The benign mix also spans low-rate trickle and moderate transfers, plus the
Section~\ref{sec:testbed} background.) The only systematic separator between the attack and its nearest
benign class is again the axis the evasion manipulates---here, throughput. The exfiltration dataset
comprises 1{,}440 flows (1{,}080 benign, 360 exfil) from 180 scenario runs, split 70/15/15 and stratified.

\paragraph{Query topology.} Unlike a beacon's, an exfiltration flow's features depend directly on link
contention: in the training scenario the exfil shares the capture host's 10\,Mbit/s link with the benign
bulk transfers. Exfil queries are therefore run \emph{inside the full training scenario}, with its
servers and background traffic; background parameters are drawn once per instance seed, so every
throttle level of an instance sees the same background. The controlled exfil sends to its own
collector port, so the delivery check counts only its bytes. (An earlier version run in a two-host
topology produced naive exfil flows faster and more regular than any training exfil, which two
detectors extrapolated to ``benign''; matching the query and training conditions removed this
artifact, and we report only the full-topology results.) We sweep three volumes ($0.8$, $1.15$, $1.5$\,MB)
with ten seeds each ($n=30$) at a naive rate of $600$\,KB/s.

\paragraph{Evasion axis and functional oracle.} The evasion axis is \textbf{throttle} $c\ge 0$: the
attacker lowers the transfer rate to push \texttt{bytes\_per\_second} out of the high-rate band. The
effective rate is
\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{eff}} \;=\; \frac{r_{0}}{1+c},
\label{eq:throttle}
\end{equation}
so $c=0$ is the naive dump and larger $c$ is slower and stealthier; the line search of
Section~\ref{sec:evasion} sweeps $c$ upward for the minimal evading value, exactly as for jitter. The
structural difference from C2 is the oracle. Raising jitter is free, but throttling stretches the
transfer and the payload must still arrive in time. Writing $b(t)$ for the cumulative bytes delivered
by time $t$, with volume $V$, deadline $D$, and transfer start $t_0$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Func}_{\mathrm{exfil}} \;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\exists\, t \le t_0 + D :\; b(t) \ge V\,\right],
\label{eq:exfil-oracle}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the full payload must complete within the deadline ($D=60$\,s), with delivery confirmed at the
collector. Throttle too far and the transfer cannot finish---an inadmissible perturbation---so, unlike
C2, the functional constraint can in principle \emph{defeat} the evasion.

\paragraph{Defense for exfiltration.} Adversarial training follows Section~\ref{sec:defense-method}:
functional throttled-exfil flows from 21 instances are folded into training as exfil, and the 9 held-out
instances are re-scored. To test what a hardened detector has learned, we additionally run escalation
sweeps on volumes unseen in hardening, varying one further attacker-controlled transfer parameter at a
time (chunk jitter, chunk size).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report, in order: clean detection performance (\ref{sec:clean}); problem-space evasion via timing
jitter (\ref{sec:jitter}); the paired classical-vs-quantum comparison and transferability
(\ref{sec:paired}); the adversarial-training defense with its axis escalation (\ref{sec:defense}); and
the same analyses for data exfiltration (\ref{sec:exfil-results}). Unless stated otherwise, evasion
rates are detected-only (Section~\ref{sec:evasion}) and written as evaded/detected.
Rates in the evasion tables carry Wilson 95\% confidence intervals; with $n\le 30$ they are wide, and we read only differences that survive them.

\subsection{Clean detection performance}
\label{sec:clean}
On unmodified traffic the detectors are accurate but \emph{not} saturated, as intended by the realistic
benign mix (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}). On the held-out test set (189 flows, 54 of them beacons;
Table~\ref{tab:clean}) all five learned detectors land within two points of each other, at
84--86\% accuracy with 87--96\% beacon recall. The VQC, at 84.1\%, is indistinguishable from the
classical models at this sample size. (On an earlier, one-third-size version of the dataset it reached
only 74.6\%; its deficit there was a small-data effect.) The single-feature \texttt{cv\_iat} rule
catches almost every beacon but flags half of all benign flows, so C2 detection genuinely needs more
than timing regularity. The 16--19\% benign false-positive rates of the learned detectors are the honest
cost of the task: the rigid benign traffic (NTP/health-check analogues) is nearly indistinguishable from
a beacon at the flow level, so a detector that catches rigid beacons necessarily mislabels some rigid
benign flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:overlap}). This is the realistic operating point a flow-level C2 detector
faces, and the regime in which we evaluate evasion.

\subsection{Problem-space evasion via timing jitter}
\label{sec:jitter}
From the capture-once sweep (Section~\ref{sec:paired-method}) we extract, for each detector, the
minimal jitter at which the beacon is classified benign while remaining functional, over the 30 beacon
instances (three check-in intervals $\times$ ten seeds). The functional oracle confirmed that
\emph{every} captured configuration remained operational ($\geq 2$ successful check-ins, no gap
$> 30$\,s): evasion is functionality-preserving throughout.

Every detector is evaded for every beacon it detected---the single exception is one VQC instance---(Table~\ref{tab:jitter}), at a cost-free level of
timing jitter: the MLPs within $(0.25, 0.3]$---at most $\approx\pm30\%$ randomization of an
otherwise-regular check-in interval---and the reference detectors already within $(0.175, 0.25]$, so
neither tree ensemble is harder to evade than the MLP. The evasion holds at every operating point: the
native MLP is evaded within $(0.275, 0.35]$ at the $2$\,s interval and within $(0.225, 0.275]$ at $4$ and
$8$\,s. Two detectors do not flag every \emph{unmodified} beacon: the VQC and the Random Forest miss all
ten rigid beacons at the $2$\,s interval, which lies at the lower edge of the training distribution (the
1st percentile of C2 inter-arrival times is 2.1\,s), while detecting every beacon at $4$ and $8$\,s. In
feature space, raising jitter walks the beacon out of its low-regularity corner into the
benign-populated region (Fig.~\ref{fig:decisionspace}). Randomizing check-in timing by this amount is a
change real C2 frameworks expose as a configuration option~\cite{cobaltstrike_beacon}, and the beacon continues to reach its C2 throughout. A detector that performs well on
clean traffic is therefore defeated, every time, by a change that costs the attacker essentially
nothing.

\subsection{Classical vs.\ quantum: paired comparison and transferability}
\label{sec:paired}
Because the quantum and classical detectors are trained on identical inputs
(Section~\ref{sec:detectors}) and scored on the \emph{same} captured flows via the capture-once sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:paired-method}), their robustness is directly comparable.

\paragraph{No consistent quantum robustness difference.} On the 19 instances that both the matched MLP
and the VQC detected and that both were evaded on, the VQC required more jitter in 9, the MLP in 7, and
they tied at grid resolution in 3. With clean accuracy also matched (Table~\ref{tab:clean}), there is no
evidence that the quantum classifier is harder---or easier---to evade with realizable traffic.

\paragraph{Transferability.} Of the minimal-jitter evasions crafted against the native MLP, 50\% (10/20)
also evade the VQC on instances the VQC detected; of those crafted against the VQC, 84\% (16/19) also
evade the MLP (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). An evasion found against one detector therefore frequently,
but not always, carries to the other; because the grid is coarse, a beacon tuned to \emph{just} cross
one threshold may sit just short of the other, and one further grid step evades both.

Single-axis adversarial training therefore does not close the vulnerability: it suppresses the axis it
targets, and the attacker relocates to a functionally-free axis it does not. Because realizable traffic
offers several such axes (timing and size here, among others), defending them one at a time is a losing
exchange---the attacker steps to an undefended axis at negligible cost.

\paragraph{Takeaway.} Across a second, structurally different attack---different detectable signal
(throughput vs.\ timing), different evasion axis (throttle vs.\ jitter), and a real time cost to the
evasion that C2 lacked---the conclusions hold: every flow-level detector, learned or rule-based,
classical or quantum, is evaded by a realizable, functionality-preserving perturbation; the cost to the
attacker is seconds, not a failed attack; and adversarial training only defends the specific
configuration it was trained on.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The weakness is in the features, not the model}
Six detectors with very different inductive biases---a neural network, a quantum circuit, two tree
ensembles and a one-line rule---fail in the same way, which points to what they share: the flow
features. Each detector separates attack from benign largely through traffic shape that the attacker
sets, timing regularity for C2 and throughput for exfiltration. The exfiltration results make this
explicit: a single cut on \texttt{bytes\_per\_second} matches the learned detectors on clean traffic,
so their decision effectively reduces to the one quantity throttling changes. C2 detection is genuinely
multi-feature (the \texttt{cv\_iat} rule alone flags half of all benign flows), yet a single
attacker-controlled axis still defeats it. A detection signal the attacker controls for free does not
hold up against an attacker who knows it is used. The benign traffic that shares the attack's shape
explains why a defender cannot simply tighten the threshold: our realism check finds about one real
benign flow in eight as regular as a beacon, and pushing the decision boundary into that region cost
false positives wherever hardening reached it (the VQC's rose from 0.17 to 0.24, the throughput rule's
from 0.16 to 0.27). Flow-shape
features remain useful against unsophisticated attacks, but against an adaptive attacker a defender
needs signals that cannot be changed without breaking the attack or paying a real cost, such as
destination reputation, behaviour aggregated over many windows, or host telemetry. We did not evaluate
such signals here.

\subsection{Why the quantum classifier offers no robustness advantage}
On C2, with three times the data of our first runs, the VQC matched the classical detectors on clean
accuracy (84.1\% against 84--86\%) and was neither consistently harder nor easier to evade on identical
flows. On exfiltration it was the weaker detector and the easier target. Robustness claims for quantum
classifiers concern small, $L_p$-bounded perturbations of the encoded input~\cite{west2023nature}; a
problem-space attacker does not make small feature-space changes. A modest, free change in timing jitter
produces a large change in \texttt{cv\_iat}, the feature that carries the detection signal, and no
model class is robust to a large change in the feature its decision rests on. The VQC's earlier deficit
on a one-third-size dataset is also a methodological caution: robustness comparisons between model
families should be made where clean detection is neither saturated nor data-starved, or differences in
evasion threshold may reflect the training regime rather than the architecture.

\subsection{Adversarial training learns the configuration, not the attack}
Hardening worked on the axis it targeted: held-out jitter evasion fell from 100\% to 0\% for the MLPs and
tree ensembles. But a second free axis, payload-size padding, re-broke every hardened C2 detector in
two to six queries. Exfiltration sharpened the point. After hardening on throttled exfiltration, Random
Forest and XGBoost resisted throttling even at rates inside the benign trickle band, and their feature
importances had shifted toward packet-size and volume features---exactly the parameters our sweep held
fixed. Changing the transfer volume alone defeated the hardened XGBoost; adding chunk-timing jitter
defeated both ensembles; and larger chunks returned every hardened learned detector to roughly its
unhardened evasion cost. Adversarial training on samples from one attack configuration thus teaches a
flexible model that configuration, and the attacker has more free parameters than the defender has
trained on. An effective defense would have to cover the joint space of attacker-controllable
parameters, and our results suggest the cost of doing so: every axis hardened pushes the boundary
further into regular or high-rate benign traffic, so we expect joint hardening to compound the
false-positive cost. Testing that is the most important open question this work raises.

\subsection{A methodological lesson: queries must match training conditions}
Our first exfiltration sweep ran evasion queries in a two-host network without background traffic. The
resulting naive exfiltration flows were faster and more regular than any training exfiltration, and
two detectors with test recall of 0.94 and above called most of them benign---an apparent evasion at zero
cost that was entirely an artifact of the query conditions. Running queries inside the training
scenario, with its background traffic, removed it. Problem-space evaluations generate their own test
traffic, and that traffic must be in-distribution for the detector at the unperturbed point; otherwise
the measured evasion conflates the attack with a train/test mismatch. Reporting the naive detection rate
next to every evasion rate, as we do, makes such mismatches visible.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Emulated testbed and synthetic benign traffic.} All traffic is generated in Mininet. Our
        benign mix is about $3.4\times$ denser in the beacon timing band than the real reference host
        (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}), so absolute false-positive rates likely overstate those of a
        deployment; the evasion results do not depend on this density.
  \item \emph{C2 query topology.} C2 queries ran in a two-host topology. This makes query beacons more
        regular, hence easier to detect, than training beacons, so the reported C2 evasion thresholds
        are conservative; we did not rerun them in the full topology.
  \item \emph{Small held-out sets.} Hardening is evaluated on 9 held-out instances per family and each
        exfiltration escalation sweep on 6, so those rates carry wide confidence intervals; we report
        them as a targeted test of the explanation rather than as precise rates.
  \item \emph{Two attack families, binary tasks.} Attacks whose signal is bound to their function, such
        as volumetric denial of service, may be harder to evade without losing effect.
  \item \emph{Simulated, single-architecture VQC.} The VQC runs on a noiseless state-vector simulator
        with one circuit design (8 qubits, 4 entangling layers); other encodings or hardware noise
        could behave differently.
  \item \emph{Weak attacker and fixed window.} The attacker is a black-box, one-axis grid search with no
        model knowledge, so our results are a lower bound on what an adaptive attacker achieves.
        Features are computed over a fixed 60\,s window; detectors aggregating behaviour over longer
        horizons were not evaluated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We evaluated flow-level intrusion detectors against problem-space evasion, in which every perturbation
is sent as real traffic and admitted only if the attack keeps working. Across C2 beaconing and data
exfiltration, six detectors---classical, tree-based, rule-based and quantum---were evaded for
essentially every attack instance they detected, at the cost of modest check-in jitter or a few seconds
of extra transfer time. The quantum classifier offered no consistent robustness advantage on matched
inputs. Adversarial training closed the axis it was trained on but defended only the trained
configuration: one further attacker-controlled parameter restored evasion at close to the original
cost. These results locate the weakness in flow features the attacker controls, not in the model class,
and show that hardening one axis at a time does not repair it. Future work should test joint
multi-axis adversarial training and its false-positive cost, extend the evaluation to attacks whose
signal is bound to their function, and examine whether features an attacker cannot change cheaply give
flow-level detectors durable robustness.
